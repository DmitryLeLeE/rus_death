METHOD — BEFORE THE IMAGES

HOW TO
READ THIS
ATLAS

This atlas was built in a fixed order: research first, then graphic interpretation, then the digital object. The method is printed because the limits of the evidence are part of the publication.

01

What this atlas is

A collection of research plates (15 at present) on death in Russian culture, arranged as an atlas rather than as a book: each plate is a composition on one problem, and the index, map and chronology provide routes between them that are not sequential.

The subject is the material and institutional record, not death as a mood: how bodies were handled, how cemeteries were regulated, how the dead were fed and named, how the state counted them, how artists used them.

02

What ‘Russian’ means here

It means the tradition of the Russian state and the Russian-language sources, centred on the territory of the Russian Federation and on the imperial and Soviet institutions that shaped it. It does not mean ‘East Slavic’.

Ukrainian, Belarusian and other traditions appear only where a source documents them, and are labelled. The one photographic example from outside the Russian Federation — a commemoration in Ukraine — is on plate D, and the caption says why it is there and what it must not be used to prove.

03

Periods, and the refusal of continuity

The atlas distinguishes four rough periods: pre-imperial (to about 1700), imperial (1700–1917), Soviet (1917–1991) and post-Soviet (1991–). These are institutional markers, not a claim that everyone in a period believed the same thing.

The same object can mean different things in different periods, and a practice recorded in 1890 is not evidence for 1490. The chronology marks three caesuras — 1917, the 1930s and 1991 — where the institutions producing the record changed. Continuity is not assumed across them.

04

Sources, and the refusal of decoration

Priority is given to academic monographs, ethnographic collections, museum and institutional records, and primary texts. Popular websites and press reports appear only where they are the best available account of a specific documented detail, and are marked as such.

No laments, proverbs or epitaphs are quoted verbatim in this edition. A quotation that cannot be checked against a printed text and defended in translation is a decoration, and decoration is the one thing this subject does not need.

05

Geography

The map plots only places for which the atlas has a documentary basis, and it does not shade areas. A photograph of one cemetery is not a distribution; a set of reports gathered from many provinces does not establish a custom’s range.

Where a source gives only a province, the entry says ‘province’. Where a location is approximate, the coordinates are approximate. One locality lies outside the Russian Federation and is marked.

06

Language

All visible text is English. Russian terms appear in simplified transliteration — kh, zh, ch, shch, y — with a gloss at first use. The apostrophe marks softness only where it is needed to avoid ambiguity.

All translations from Russian are working translations made for this edition unless a published English translation is named. Dates are given as in the source; no attempt is made to harmonise Old and New Style across the atlas.

07

The material layer is an argument

The torn edges, toner, halftone screens and photocopy damage in this publication are not a mood laid over the content. They say something about the evidence itself: it gets reproduced, damaged, recopied and is never complete.

The contrast between clean and damaged surfaces is deliberate. Some plates are almost clean because the material they present is confident; others are dense with damage because the record is.

No single overlay is applied to the whole site. Each texture has a role: a dirty photocopy for a print-derived image, paper grain for an editorial page, a halftone screen for a painting that reached most viewers as a reproduction.

08

What is missing

There is no field survey, no municipal burial register and no systematic study of post-Soviet commercial funerals in this edition. The digital memorial is recorded as a research question, not as a finding.

The atlas is published with these gaps visible. A reader who wants to extend it should start with the bibliography’s limitation notes: each one says what the source cannot support.

THE THREE LEVELS

DOCUMENTED A fact, practice or object that a cited source records.

The source, its date and its limitations are in the bibliography. ‘Documented’ does not mean ‘universal’: a documented practice may be one village’s practice.

INTERPRETATION An argument about what the documentation means.

Includes the atlas’s own readings, and the arguments of the scholars cited. Interpretations are attributed where they belong to someone, and marked as editorial where they belong to this edition.

RECONSTRUCTION A drawing, diagram, sequence or layout made for this atlas.

Reconstructions are never presented as historical photographs. Diagrams of ritual sequences, cross types and cemetery plans are analytical drawings, not records of a specific event.
