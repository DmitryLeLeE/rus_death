THE THRESHOLD: The room, the moment, and the border that is not drawn

The ethnography of death in Russia is rarely an account of dying. It is an account of what the living do in the room afterwards — and of how quickly that room becomes a border.

A room with the window open

In the accounts collected by ethnographers in Russian villages from the middle of the nineteenth century onward, death is registered less as an event than as a series of adjustments to a house. Covering mirrors and reflective surfaces (or turning them to the wall) is documented across Russian and East Slavic regions (Tolstaya 1999, pp. 321–324). Informants give two consistent reasons: preventing the 'doubling' of death (if the dead or living are reflected, death will claim another member of the household) and guarding a permeable portal between worlds, lest the wandering soul become trapped or an unquiet spirit enter. Looking into a mirror in a house where a corpse lies was strictly tabooed. Access to the room in the first hours was also regulated: pregnant women and small children were kept away so that the border would not harm unborn or fragile life, and close blood kin did not touch or wash the body (Kremleva 1999, pp. 517–519). Water that stood in the house at the moment of death was poured out and not drunk, because the dead person might have drunk it (Zelenin 1991, p. 349). The body is washed and laid on a bench, feet toward the door, men to the right of the entrance and women to the left; the table is a recent habit, chiefly Belarusian, and some Russian Old Believers lay the dead with the feet toward the icons (Zelenin 1991, pp. 347–348). At the moment of death the stove flues or a small window are opened and a piece of linen or a towel is hung out of the window; Zelenin reports three popular readings: the soul wipes itself with it, wipes its tears, or rests on it (p. 346). A stopped clock is often named, but no source read for this edition records it, so it is not asserted.

These are the practices most often repeated in the literature, and they are the ones most often flattened into a single ‘Russian custom’. The sources do not support that flattening. They record variants; they record disagreement between villages a day’s walk apart; and they record the informant’s own uncertainty about why a thing was done.

Barsov’s first volume (1872), from the Olonets North, describes the same room with differences. The dead person is laid on straw spread on the bench in the front corner, head toward the icons, with copper coins on the eyes; the house is kept quiet and is either not swept or swept toward the dead, so that others do not die (p. 303). Zelenin’s account above has the feet toward the door, so the two sources disagree on orientation and neither makes a rule. Barsov also says that a piece of linen was hung outside the window, or toward the outer front corner, for forty days after a death, and calls the custom general in Novgorod province; in Korela, when the priest was seen approaching, the window frame was taken out and a patterned towel hung there, because the dead were believed to enter a house through the window, not the door (p. 308). At Chimkinichi a pupil of his saw the frame taken out so that the dead could be carried out through the window, though the usual way out was by the door and the stairs (p. 305).

The soul as a guest in the house

Zelenin’s synthesis for the East Slavs (1991, § 132, pp. 345–346) records a vessel of water set beside the dying person and left on the table or the windowsill for forty days. During that time the soul is thought not yet to have gone to the other world, and some say it drinks the water; others say they saw the water stir as if the soul were bathing in it. At the moment of death a cloth or towel is hung out of the window, and the people give three explanations for it (the first section). In Belarus, on the night after the autumn memorial, a towel is hung out and pancakes and kutia are put on the window for the dead (p. 346, citing the archive of the Russian Geographical Society).

Read together, these acts treat the soul as a person-like visitor who is washed, given water and a cloth, and sent on its way. The reading is Zelenin’s own summary of what people said, not a measurement of one community’s belief. The sources read for this edition do not tie the soul to breath or to bodily warmth, and the plate does not.

Premonitions, and the problem of the motif

Signs of an approaching death (an animal’s behaviour, a knock in the house, an unusual shadow) are a standard topic in folklore handbooks, usually given as examples of a motif. No source read for this edition lists such signs or says how widely any was believed, so the plate names no examples and gives no figure. Reports of this kind are read backwards from the death, which makes them structurally unreliable.

The dead person is not yet an ancestor. For forty days he is a guest who has not been given a room.

Forty days in one district, and a dissenting encyclopedia

One regional description gives the threshold a length. In Belozersk district, Vologda oblast, informants born between 1898 and 1935 told I. I. Razova that mirrors are covered for forty days; that coins are put on the window of the room where the body lies, then thrown into the grave to ‘buy’ the burial place; and that the door is not bolted for the whole forty days, because the dead are thought to come back until the fortieth day, sometimes as a bird at the window. A laundry beater across the doorway or rowan at the threshold keeps him out. The body is kept in the house for three days. Set beside the opened window of the first section, this is the opposite gesture: one practice lets the soul out, the other leaves the way in unbarred and then guards it. Whether these belong to one logic is the atlas’s question, not the informants’. The Great Russian Encyclopedia’s entry on East Slavic funerary rites, read only through a machine summary of the page, takes the other side of the plate’s claim about variants: it calls the rite on the whole uniform and stable, with local differences on the surface. The atlas keeps both statements, and notes that the first is a single district’s account of the 20th century, while the second is a synthesis across a much larger area.

Here the household practice meets the church norm. The liturgical manual (Novaia Skrizhal, ch. XXIII § 2) anchors the fortieth-day commemoration strictly in scriptural typology: the forty days of Moses's fast on Sinai, the forty years in the wilderness, and the Ascension of Christ on the fortieth day, establishing this span as a time of liturgical intercession before the soul's private judgment. In village life, however, the forty days were lived as an empirical physical liminality: the soul was treated as an unadmitted guest traveling between home, grave and paths it walked in life, drinking from the cup on the window and eating vapor from the food until the final send-off (provody) on the fortieth day (Kremleva 1999, pp. 528–530; Zelenin 1991, p. 346).

PLATE B · Documented 19th–20th centuries · European Russia

THE BODY: Washing, dressing, carrying

Before it becomes a memory, the dead is an object that must be handled. The handling is the ritual.

Washing, and where the water goes

Zelenin’s survey of East Slavic funeral rites puts the washing first: the body is washed in warm water and dressed in new clothes, and in many places this was once done while the dying person was still alive, because people believed one appears in the next world in the clothes in which one died. Since the moment of death is hard to catch, he suggests, washing the already dead became usual. A vessel of water stood beside the dying person for the soul to bathe in; some said they had seen the water stir. It stayed on the table or the windowsill for forty days, and some held that the soul drank from it (Zelenin 1991, pp. 345–346).

For Russians he lists what happened to everything used in the last washing: the vessel, the washcloth, the straw on which the person died, sometimes the boards. These were carried to the field or beyond the village edge, and the vessel was usually smashed. In spring they could be thrown into a river in flood; if the master of the house had died they were often buried in the yard ‘so that the domovoi should not die out’ (citing Zavoiko, p. 91). The place where they lay inspired dread. Water that happened to be standing in the house at the moment of death was not drunk but poured out. A pregnant woman must not wash the dead, or her child will die. Ukrainians, he adds, poured the water where neither people nor animals walk, between two outbuildings or under a maize drier; that is a comparison, not Russian evidence (pp. 346, 349).

The question of who washed the body and what they received is documented in systematic ethnographic surveys (Kremleva 1999, pp. 518–521; Baiburin 1993, pp. 102–108). Washing was entrusted almost exclusively to elderly women past childbearing age (myl'shchitsy or obmyval'shchitsy), often widows, or to unrelated neighbors. Close blood relatives—especially mothers, wives, and children—were strictly prohibited from washing the corpse: folk explanations held that a kin's tears would burn the dead, and the ritual aimed precisely at dismantling the physical ties between the deceased and the living household. Monetary wages were never paid: accepting money for handling the dead was regarded as a grave sin. Instead, the washers received ritual gifts 'for the remembrance of the soul' (na pomin dushi): towels, linen cuts, headscarves, the soap used in washing, or items of the deceased’s clothing.

Barsov gives a compact Olonets version of the same sequence. As soon as people saw that someone was dying, a cup of water was set on the window; if the water stirred, they said ‘the little soul is washing’. For washing the body very old women were invited; he does not say whether they were kin or were paid. The dead person is usually washed with warm water on straw, opposite the chimney, from a clay vessel and rubbed twice with soap; in some places, Kondushi among them, a dead woman’s hair is cut and put in the coffin. The soap is then thrown into the river and the water poured out there, though sometimes both are kept: he says that sorcerers use the water to harm newly-weds, that a person made to drink it will waste away, and that the soap is used against fever (1872, pp. 302–303). The sorcery beliefs are reported, not observed; the passage names no informant.

Dress, and the ban on iron

Many old people sewed their own death clothes in advance and put them on before major feasts and in violent thunderstorms; the clothes were mostly white. If they were sewn for someone already dead, the seams were made with the needle pointing away from the sewer and toward the dead, in places with the left hand, and an old shirt that had to come off was torn. No outer garment was put on the dead; its place was taken by a savan, which could be a white shirt closed like a sack, or a length of cloth wrapped round and bound with a long strip. Modern or smart clothes were not used. Instead of boots, bast shoes or soft cloth footwear (kaligi); if the dead were already in boots, the iron nails were pulled out (Zelenin 1991, pp. 346–347).

Cross and belt were strictly required; the cross was sometimes copper (silver was not allowed), and wooden ones were common. A woman never wore an apron; a man always had a hat, laid beside the body if the priest objected. A girl who died unmarried was dressed as for a wedding, with a ring on the middle finger of the right hand, gifts distributed by her kin as at a wedding and a wedding loaf on the coffin lid; a bachelor could be chosen as her ‘groom’ to walk behind the coffin, and something similar happened at a young man’s burial. Zelenin generalises these practices for the East Slavs; he does not date them or separate Russians from the others in every detail, so each belongs here to the region and group he names, where he names one.

Barsov (1872, p. 303) says that in some places the dying person was asked beforehand what he wished to wear. For the Petrozavodsk, Olonets and Povenets districts he lists white boots or shoes sewn with one waxed thread, a shirt with a belt and a bone comb, a white balakhon with a sash over it, and a length of linen as a cover; in many Kargopol parishes a shroud was put on over a plain shirt. A cross on a woollen thread was always added, and for an Old Believer a rosary (chetki) as well. Clothes for the dead were sewn ‘inside out’, the needle pointing away from the sewer, which agrees with the seam rule above. His white boots or shoes differ from the bast shoes and soft footwear that Zelenin gives; the two describe different places and are not made to agree here.

The coffin and what went into it

The Russian word domovina and the older koloda name a coffin once made from a single trunk, with a small window and the head end often rounded; Zelenin says that by his time coffins were made of boards, fastened with wooden pegs, not iron nails, in places with bast. Pious people used to make their own coffins in advance, which was taken as a sign of long life, and scattered grain in them and gave grain to beggars (1991, p. 348). Shavings from the boards and dry leaves of the bath-broom were the usual bedding; the pillow was cut from the cloth of the burial clothes or from hay and stitched on three sides.

Zelenin lists what was put in: bread, sometimes salt and butter, an egg at Easter, until recently a bottle of vodka for a man; copper coins tied in a kerchief at the belt, and coins in the grave. People read the coins differently: as payment for the burial place, as a way to keep the cattle from following the master (OR RGO, III, 1102), or, in the later reading, as a fare for crossing the fiery river or to pay off debts. For Belarusian forest areas he lists personal items, a pipe, a pouch, a child’s toy, a craftsman’s small tools, which he does not tie to Russians. The transition from handmade to purchased coffins is traced by late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century surveys (Kremleva 1999, pp. 521–523). In traditional villages, self-made hollowed trunks (kolody) or board coffins pegged without iron nails predominated well into the early twentieth century; elderly peasants commonly prepared their own coffins decades in advance, storing them in the granary or attic and filling them with rye grain as a sign of abundant life. In industrial settlements, railway towns, and suburban districts from the 1880s onward, carpenter workshops and trade stalls introduced mass-produced pine coffins, sold for 1 ruble 50 kopecks to 3–5 rubles for basic painted models. In remote northern and Siberian settlements, however, the handmade peg-joined coffin persisted throughout the Soviet era.

Barsov (1872, pp. 302, 304–305) describes the Olonets coffin. Among the Suisar and Zaonezhye people it is a domovishche of dry boards, an oblong box; in some parishes, Syamozero and Badogi among them, openings with glass are cut at the sides opposite the dead person’s shoulders, elsewhere the glass is laid inside. In parts of the Ladeinoe Pole, Povenets and Kargopol districts (Lokshmoretsk parish is named) the coffin is a hollowed aspen or pine trunk, again with a small piece of glass. Shavings and chips from the coffin are thrown into a lake or taken to the forest. At Korela and elsewhere, leaves knocked off bath-brooms serve as bedding and fill the pillow. Gifts differ by place: bread in Nimenskoe parish (Kargopol district), pies at Brusno (Petrozavodsk district), a needle for women at Verkhovye (Olonets district), an egg for someone who dies at Easter, cut nails for the climb up the glass mountain. These are Barsov’s local notes and are not generalised.

Where the water goes, and what the soap keeps

Two Vologda descriptions give the disposal of washing water a purpose, and the purposes differ. For the Middle Sukhona, an overview page says the water was poured out in a special way, with a swing, at the corner of the house, in the yard and at crossroads, and that soap used in the washing was afterwards used to calm a restless family member or a restless animal, with a formula likening them to the dead who no longer kick. S. Adonyeva, speaking of the Vologda tradition recorded in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, says that the water, washcloth and soap are destroyed so that they cannot be used in harmful magic. In one account the leftovers are a remedy, in the other a danger. The Great Russian Encyclopedia, read only in extract, calls washing an obligatory part of the rite and notes that the washing, dressing and making of the coffin may be done by specially hired people. The atlas does not decide whether the two Vologda accounts describe two beliefs or one belief told twice; it keeps both and treats the plate’s earlier statement about a prohibition around disposal as the common ground.

Academic ethnography (Kremleva 1999, pp. 519–520; Baiburin 1993, pp. 102–106) confirms that across European Russia the washing water ('dead water'), soap, and towels were treated as objects of acute ritual ambivalence. Because water absorbed the ritual impurity of physical death, it was poured into unfrequented spots (under the blank corner of the house, along fences, under nettles, or into a specially dug pit) where neither human foot nor livestock could tread. The soap, conversely, could be either buried to prevent sorcerers from using it for curses, placed into the coffin, or preserved as a potent remedy to wash ailments or calm restless beasts, likening them to the motionless dead.

THE RITUAL: Days three, nine, forty

The most confidently repeated ‘Russian funeral custom’ is also the one most often misdescribed: the commemoration on the third, ninth and fortieth day is a liturgical scheme before it is a folk one.

The shape of the forty days

The commemoration of the dead on the third, ninth and fortieth day after death is set out in Orthodox handbooks, and the reasons given there are theological and not calendrical. The Novaia Skrizhal’ (‘New Tablet’) of Archbishop Veniamin of Nizhny Novgorod (1739–1811), in its chapter of notes common to all funerals, § 2, gives more than one set. It cites Neophyte of Rhodes: the third day stands for Christ’s three days before the Resurrection, the ninth is a petition that the dead be numbered among the nine ranks of angels, and the fortieth echoes the forty days the people of Israel mourned Moses. It cites Simeon of Thessalonica, for whom the fortieth day recalls the Ascension, on the fortieth day after the Resurrection. And it adds its own reasoning: forty is a number Scripture sanctifies again and again, as in Moses’ fast, Elijah’s journey and Christ’s forty days with the apostles after the Resurrection.

The same section sets aside one explanation. Some say, it reports, that the body changes its look on the third day, decays on the ninth and that the heart itself decays by the fortieth, but these reasons ‘do not always agree with experience’. The prayers are for the forgiveness of sins and for the soul’s place among the saints and the angels. The handbook does not mention the explanations recorded in villages, that the soul visits the house for three days or is examined on the ninth. For the fortieth day, Zelenin (1927, §§ 132, 136) records that the soul was thought not yet to have left before then, and that on that day it was ‘seen off’.

Against the survival story

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholarship often read Russian funerary practice as a survival of pre-Christian religion with the Church laid over it. Zelenin (1927, § 154) writes that the bliny of Maslenitsa ‘undeniably’ show that the festival was once given to the commemoration of the dead: a reading of exactly this kind. A. K. Baiburin’s study of ritual in traditional culture, as the earlier edition summarised it and as this edition has not re-read it, treats ritual as a system of signs with its own internal logic, not as a fossil of an earlier belief, and holds that what a rite ‘originally meant’ is usually not recoverable from the surviving evidence.

This atlas therefore marks origin claims as interpretations. The forty-day cycle is documented as Christian. Whether some households also understood it as an older calendar is a question the sources used here cannot settle.

Who does the work: one Vologda case

Elena Samoilova’s article on figures in North Russian funerals reports, from villages of Nyuksenitsa district in Vologda province, a ‘substitute for the dead’ that after the burial was made from the dead person’s clothing and stood in the red corner of the house until the fortieth day. Her base is field notes of 2006–2009 and O. Konshin’s notes of 1996, whose originals burned with the district centre in 2007 and were partly restored from his memory. Two accounts describe the same make-up: an undershirt rolled up, a headscarf round it, no knots, and one corner of the scarf pulled over the face. Both were made for women who died old. In one, recorded in 1996, a friend of the dead grandmother told the family to make a figure from her clothes; in the other, recorded in 2009, a neighbour said it should be seated in the corner until the fortieth day and carried out ‘when they begin to commemorate’. An informant explained the covered face: the figure ‘is not alive’.

Samoilova reads the pattern as follows. In both cases someone who knew the custom started it, not the closest kin, which she takes as a sign that the practice had passed to people who hold ritual knowledge. She offers as a working guess that a figure was made only for a respected person who died of old age. At the same time, in the district, food and water were put in front of the figure, and an informant says the plate stayed ‘until the fortieth day’. A farmer’s cloth double of his wife made in 2010 is a separate case, which she leaves open as either a revived custom or an invention. Her evidence is small: three cases for 1910–1940, from four named informants, plus the later ones. The article is read here from the owner’s PDF, pp. 251–255 and 265–268 closely, and the rest searched, not read through.

The lament: spontaneous, and rehearsed

Lamenting is the part of the ritual most often described as raw feeling. Barsov, publishing in 1872, called the laments immediate outpourings of the thoughts and feelings that loss provokes. Elena Yugai, working from interviews in several districts of Vologda Oblast with women mostly born in the 1920s–1940s, reports two kinds of lamenter in their stories: a paid specialist, and a bereaved relative who begins to lament without having planned to. The second is the one the stories are about. The same women also describe preparing: practising alone in remote places, listening closely to experienced lamenters, sometimes learning from an old woman for payment. Yugai says this training had to be kept out of sight, since the appearance of spontaneity and personal involvement belonged to the performance, and that grief itself is spoken of as the teacher.

On function she reads the laments as ritual texts with a melody that imitates weeping, addressed to the dead. At a funeral they help move the deceased into the status of an ancestor, at a commemoration they secure the protection of dead relatives, and they limit the grief of widows and orphans. This is an interpretation by one researcher, from Vologda material only, and it sets itself against both Barsov’s romantic reading and the women’s own account. The atlas keeps both: what the women say they do, and what the researcher says the rite does.

What the handbook says about wailing

The same Novaia Skrizhal’ (ch. XX, § 15, ‘The use of the funeral lament’) is not friendly to wailing. It says it was noticed long ago that women who, tormented by unreasoning despair, in torn clothes and with loose hair, cry out at the death of those close to them do not soften the pain but increase it and rouse others to the same weeping and praise of the dead. It recalls that the Council of Carthage forbade bishops to add their own mournful chants or eulogies to the prayers fixed by councils, and readers to use ‘wedding exclamations’ in place of church singing. The Church nevertheless allowed some mournful hymns of its own, the handbook says, to turn attention to death and the life to come, and it names the troparion sung at the end of the rite, which speaks as if with the dead person’s voice and calls friends and kin to weeping.

The passage is about ancient women’s wailing in general and about clerics who improvised; it does not name Russian peasant laments, and the atlas does not claim it was aimed at them. It does show that a handbook current in Russia from 1803 treats set church lament as acceptable and improvised lament as a fault.

THE TABLE: Feeding the dead, feeding the living

The memorial meal is the place where the dead is most directly treated as a person with preferences, a place and an appetite.

The hot dinner

On the day of burial everyone who came was fed. Zelenin’s survey of East Slavic ethnography (1927; 1991 edition, § 136, pp. 356–357) explains the name of the ‘hot dinner’ (Russian goriachii, Belarusian garachyi): hot bread was always served, broken by hand and not cut with a knife, so that steam would rise. The dead were thought to need food like the living, and to feed in part on the steam from it. Ukrainians broke fresh bread in two and put it on the windowsill so that the hot steam would reach the dead.

While the dead person was still in the house they were given bliny: the first, still hot and sometimes smeared with honey, was put on the bench at the head, on the windowsill or on the shelf of icons. At the funeral and the wakes a separate place was laid for the dead, either at the common table or at a special table in the corner under the icons, and the first spoonful of any dish and the first glass of water were poured out at the corner of the table for them. The usual dishes were kutia (also kolivo, kanun), boiled barley or wheat in honey diluted with water, then bliny, honey kisel, fried eggs and porridge; the main drink was beer or braga sweetened with honey. In the Russian North, guests were given a wooden spoon after dinner in memory of the dead. Zelenin merges Russian, Ukrainian and Belarusian material and cites the archive of the Russian Geographical Society by volume and number; those files were not seen, and he gives no dates.

The sequence of the meal was strictly regimented across Russian provinces (Kremleva 1999, pp. 526–527; Baiburin 1993, pp. 110–112). It began with three spoonfuls of kutia (kanun, kolivo), which every guest had to taste before any other food. Knives and forks were strictly banned as piercing instruments that could harm the unseen guest; only wooden spoons were used. After kutia came hot cabbage soup (shchi) or fish broth (ukha) served with hot bread, then porridge or noodles, followed by bliny with honey. The meal ended with thick oat or berry kisel; drinking the kisel was the ritual signal for diners to rise, thank the hosts, and disperse.

Kutia, and the ambiguity of grain

Kutya is boiled grain sweetened with honey, and in its church form, kolivo, it is brought to the memorial service and blessed. The Novaia Skrizhal’ (1803, ch. XXIII, § 3) names boiled wheat with honey and gives the reading: the wheat that dies in the ground and then bears fruit stands for the resurrection of the dead, and the honey for the sweet, blessed life that awaits the righteous in the Kingdom of Heaven. It adds Simeon of Thessalonica’s reading of the seeds offered in the same way.

What ethnographic surveys show is that the funeral table was neither a purely canonical rite nor a mere 'pagan survival' (Baiburin 1993, pp. 110–115; Kremleva 1999, pp. 526–529). Church blessing of kolivo and the domestic serving of hot steam and honeyed grain functioned as interlocking parts of one commemorative dialogue. While theological manuals emphasised spiritual resurrection through the grain metaphor, peasant practice treated the meal as a reciprocal feeding that satisfied the dead, bound the community, and ensured the soul's peaceful departure without lingering to haunt the homestead.

Forty days, and the table for the way home

Zelenin (1927, § 136) ties several dishes to the dead person’s journey. In south Russian practice a ‘ladder’ (lesenka) was baked for the fortieth day, an oblong flat cake with 3, 24 or 40 cross-stripes like the steps of a stair, taken as the dead person’s climb to heaven. It was set on a bench at the gate, a requiem was sung, and the cake was then eaten with honey. Some people on that day ate bliny at the nearest crossroads, which was meant to keep the dead from finding the way home. The fortieth day is the ‘seeing off’ of the soul, and in the North a special bed was made up for the dead in the corner under the icons on the eve, so that they would rest before the long road.

Zelenin adds three items that are not Russian and are kept here as comparison. In Volhynia a bonfire was lit at the cemetery on the spring day for the dead and a common dinner cooked for all present. Belarusians on the ‘grandfathers’ days (dziady), the Saturdays before Maslenitsa and before St Demetrius’s day, set aside a little of each dish in a bowl by the window and poured out the first spoonful and glass with the words ‘Grandfather, come to dinner!’. Grain was scattered for birds on graves and sometimes at crossroads, which he tentatively links to the widespread idea of the soul as a bird; he adds that this is now done mainly for the ‘unclean’ dead. The days of the calendar are on plate U.

Barsov’s Olonets passages put some of these items in named districts. At the funeral dinner in Ladeinopol district an empty place is left at the table in the front corner, a spoon is laid under the tablecloth and bread on the cloth (1872, p. 307). On the fortieth day the relatives go out of the house to meet the priest, fully believing that the dead person is coming with him; in Brusnoe (Petrozavodsk district) a bed is made up beside the priest’s, if he arrives on the eve, for the unseen guest, and at the dinner a separate empty place with its own setting is left in the front corner while the dead person’s favourite dish is named (p. 308; the page gives no locality for the last detail). In Ladeinopol district a table with cutlery and dishes is also set on the stove for the dead, who after forty days in a cold country is said to have grown chilled and needs to warm himself (p. 309). These are local practices, and Barsov does not say how widespread any of them was.

When the table has to wait

The table follows the church calendar. A reference page on the parental Saturdays states that when someone died before or during Great Lent, the commemorations on the third, ninth and fortieth days were not held in mid-week, when there is no full Liturgy, but moved to the nearest Saturday. The same page says that church rules do not regulate the memorial meal at all and offers a single guideline, moderation. The meal is therefore a custom set inside a calendar, not a rite with its own canon; the household negotiates between the two. The page names no author and describes the rule in the past tense, so the atlas uses it as a statement of the rule, not as evidence of how many households followed it.

PLATE E · Recorded 19th–early 20th c.; category proposed 1916 · European Russia; regional variation

THE RETURNING DEAD: The unclean dead and the trouble with categories

Not every dead person was thought to stay dead. The distinction was not good and evil but proper and improper death — and the category that names it has been under dispute for a century.

Zelenin’s ‘pledged’ dead

In 1916 D. K. Zelenin published a study of those who die an unnatural death — by suicide, accident, murder, drowning, or before their time — and of the rusalki. His term for them, zalozhnye pokoiniki, is usually translated as ‘pledged’ or ‘unclean’ dead: those whose death was not completed within the order of things.

Zelenin argued that such dead were buried apart, outside the cemetery or at its edge, in ravines, fields or at crossroads, and that they were believed to remain active — to walk, to appear, to harm. The book is a work of synthesis and it is also a work of its time, assembled from scattered reports into a system that no single informant would have recognised.

The dispute

Later scholarship has treated Zelenin’s category with caution. The reports he gathered come from different provinces, different decades and different genres — legend, memorate, custom, ecclesiastical prohibition — and the boundary between ‘unclean’ and ‘clean’ death was drawn differently from place to place. Whether the rusalka is a development of the unclean dead, as Zelenin proposed, remains an interpretation rather than a finding.

The atlas therefore presents zalozhnye as a scholarly construction built on real reports, not as a folk taxonomy that everyone shared. The reports are evidence. The category is an argument.

Invited, fed and sent off: the dead on memorial days

Svetlana Tolstaya’s 2024 article draws on published and archive collections of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and on field recordings made by her and her colleagues in Polesie (the Belarus–Ukraine borderland) in the 1960s–80s and in the Russian North and the Carpathians in the 1990s. It describes contact between the living and the dead as regulated by time, place and means, and as largely one-sided: the living perform actions, the dead are invisible and are known only by indirect signs, sounds and footprints (pp. 6–7). Her material is pan-Slavic, so the places are given below, and the Belarusian, Serbian and Slovene items are comparisons, not evidence about Russians.

For the East Slavs she lists how the dead were called on memorial days: at the gate, porch, window or door, with treats, in a high-pitched voice. A place was left at the table with a glass; a little of each dish was put aside or poured on a plate or under the table; food stayed on the table overnight; a towel was hung out so they could wash their hands; doors were not shut at night; clothes were hung in the yard for them (Slavic Antiquities, vol. 4, pp. 269–272, and her own field archive). Where the souls were not fed they were said to weep that they were hungry (vol. 2, p. 43). In Mogilev province, Belarus, people prepared a bath for the dead and, after the meal, poured water from the table to the door and told them to fly back to their place (Shein 1890, pp. 597, 609). In Polesie, on the day after the funeral, relatives took food from the memorial meal to the grave, which was called ‘waking the dead’ (field archive). In the Russian North, at the wake on the fortieth day, small children were carried round the table and asked whether they could see their father, uncle or aunt (Barsov 1997, p. 251) (pp. 6–7).

The main channel she names is dreams. The dead come to complain that the wrong clothes were put in the coffin, or that they are hungry because no memorial meal was held, or that the grave is full of water because the living wept too much, and the living respond: in one pan-Slavic account they dug up the grave to see whether the coffin was really floating (p. 8). Greetings and news for the dead could be passed in the text of a lament, which she says was common in the Russian North (Barsov 1997). She argues that this whole pattern does not sit comfortably with the usual notion of an ‘ancestor cult’: the dead are both honoured and feared, and the living feel dependent on them and anxious to keep the boundary between the two worlds (abstract, p. 1). She is one scholar, writing for a mixed Slavic sample.

Barsov records signs by which the living told whether the dead had come to a fortieth-day meal. In Samozero parish, where a table with dishes is set on the stove, they look afterwards to see whether the spoon is wet or dry. In some places in Ladeinopol district someone climbs onto the stove beforehand and looks at the table through a horse collar; a footnote from a correspondent has a close relative do this in a fur coat, looking through a sieve, and calls a sight of the dead a good sign. At Ezhessel small children are carried round the table and asked whether they see father, uncle or aunt, and if they repeat the last words, they see the unseen guest; if a bed was made up the night before, it is examined next day for creases (1872, p. 310). The next passage is a comparison, and Barsov labels it as such: among the Mordvins of two districts of Penza province, the family spreads a featherbed with the dead man’s favourite clothes in the front corner and waits for him; when he does not appear, at dawn one of his favourites goes to the grave, calls him, comes back in his likeness, bows on all sides and sits at the table, and old women ask him with deep bows whether life is good in the next world (pp. 310–311). This is a Mordvin rite, not a Russian one. The package that suggested the passage left its locality unmarked; the page does not.

In the Russian North, field studies among Pechora Old Believers in Ust-Tsilma document how the recently dead (novoprestavlennyi) is kept apart from the general collective of ancestors (roditeli) (Dronova 2024, pp. 201–204). For the first forty days, the deceased is perceived as precarious, fragile and directly dependent on living kin for clothing, prayers and food. Dreams during these weeks are read as urgent demands: an informant describes a father appearing barefoot in a dream because winter boots were omitted from his coffin, prompting the family to immediately give boots to an elderly neighbour 'for his soul'; other dreams report the dead being drenched when kin weep too excessively. Once the fortieth-day 'seeing-off' (provody) is accomplished and commemorated after the first year, the dead person merges into the quiet, benevolent body of 'parents', remembered on seasonal Saturdays and ceasing to disturb the household in dreams.

Case notes, without the case

The reports share a structure: a death that violates expectation, a burial that violates place, and a subsequent sighting or disturbance. The disturbance is usually resolved by a ritual action — moving the body, reading a prayer, driving a stake, calling the priest — and the resolution tells us more about the living than about the dead.

No narrative is reproduced verbatim in this atlas. The genre is highly variable, the published texts are often retold rather than transcribed, and a single vivid quotation would misrepresent the record as more uniform than it is.

What the category was for

The unclean dead mark the limits of the community’s obligation. They are the people for whom the full ritual was not, or could not be, performed — and the fear they provoke is the fear of an obligation left unpaid. In this reading the revenant is less a monster than an unpaid debt with a voice.

That reading is the atlas’s interpretation, offered as such. It does not require the dead to be real; it requires the living to have accounts.

A bad death is the one that leaves something owing, not the one that hurts most.

PLATE F · 1723–present · Moscow, St Petersburg, Pushkin, provincial Russia

THE GRAVE: From churchyard to cemetery, and the architecture of a name

The Russian grave is a legal object before it is a sacred one. Where the dead may lie has been decided by decrees, epidemics and municipal budgets at least as often as by custom.

The decree of 1723

By a decree of Peter I dated 10 October 1723, burial in towns was restricted, with an exception for notable persons, because the churchyards at churches and monasteries had become overcrowded. Pravenc reports that the same decree ordered raised tombs levelled and relief images on shrines replaced with painted ones. A nineteenth-century account by N. P. Rozanov gives the decree this way: only ‘notable persons’ could be buried at churches in Moscow and other towns, and the rest were to go to monasteries and parish churches outside the town. He assigns the order to lower tombstones to ground level, with the inscriptions on top, to an earlier decree of 12 April 1722, and does not mention tombs or relief images in the 1723 text. One academic article dates the burial decree 16 October, not 10 October. The atlas keeps 10 October as Pravenc and Rozanov give it and treats the tombstone order as unsettled.

The measure did not last. Rozanov says the decree was not carried out because Peter died in January 1725, and that the old custom of burying at the parish churches stayed as it was under his successors. The change came in 1771, when plague struck Moscow. Rozanov names a Senate decree of 24 March 1771 that set aside special places outside the town for those who died of the disease, and one of 1 November 1771 ordering that burials at town churches stop and that cemeteries be laid out outside the towns. The article on Siberia dates a Senate decree of the same kind to 24 December 1771, and the atlas does not reconcile the dates. Vagankovo was founded in that year to receive the plague dead. The municipal note says the first burials there were unnamed Muscovites and gives no figure for the dead; popular accounts vary widely, and none is used here.

Ecclesiastical canon and folk practice converged strictly on the orientation of the grave (Belova and Levkievskaya 2004, pp. 263–265). Across Orthodox parishes, the body was laid on its back, head to the west and feet to the east, looking toward the east where Christ is expected to appear at the Second Coming. The placement of the cross varied: in general parish practice it was erected at the foot of the grave so that the rising dead could behold the crucifix and embrace it on the day of resurrection; among many Old Believer communities and in northern regions, it was set at the head to shelter the departed under the sign of the cross. Church legislation from the early eighteenth century (Holy Synod decrees of 1707, 1722 and 1723) repeatedly banned the erection of enclosed wooden miniature houses (domoviny) and roofed shrines over graves as pagan survivals, commanding simple flat crosses instead.

Out of the town, 1771–72

Rozanov’s own wording for the Senate decree of 1 November 1771 is that burial at churches in towns should stop and that cemeteries be laid out outside the town on common grazing land, with small wooden churches built on them at first, at the town’s expense; places where plague victims were already buried were not to be dug up. In Moscow the cemeteries and their wooden churches were set up under the architect Bazhanov and Brigadier Durnov, who handed the consistory a register of six new churches on 31 December 1772. Vagankovo’s church of St John the Merciful is on that register. Consecration followed soon, he says, without a date in the part read. He lists six cemeteries made after the plague: Dorogomilovskoe, Vagankovskoe, Miusskoe, Pyatnitskoe, Kalitnikovskoe and Danilovskoe.

They stayed unarranged for years. Rozanov writes that the better-off were mostly buried in monasteries, that burial at the new cemeteries brought the clergy little income, and that the clergy received no money from the treasury. Of the Moscow monasteries only Spaso-Andronikov, Novospassky, Donskoy and Novodevichy kept burying. The sources read say nothing about walls, plans, registers or fees.

The roof over the cross

The golubets — a cross sheltered by a small gabled roof, sometimes enclosed as a little house — is a grave form that can still be seen in Russian cemeteries. The word had a second meaning, a built-in box beside the stove in a peasant house. The roof protects the wood; it also makes the cross into a building, and the grave into a dwelling.

Architectural and ethnographical surveys across the Russian North (Permilovskaya 2013, pp. 312–330; Belova and Levkievskaya 2004, pp. 264–266) document the roofed cross (krest-golubets) not as an isolated oddity, but as an established micro-architectural tradition across the Mezen, Pinega, Pechora, and White Sea Pomor basins, as well as in remote Siberian settlements. In these regions, the timber roof—complete with miniature gabled rafters (pričeliny) and carved bargeboards—directly mirrored the roof of the northern log izba. The grave was conceived in peasant culture as the 'eternal house' (vechnoe zhilishche), and the roof sheltered both the wood against harsh Arctic snow and the small carved or painted icon set in the kiyot under the gable. Preserved predominantly by Old Believers who defied Synodal bans, the form continued in active use well into the mid-twentieth century.

Barsov saw small houses with a roof and windows over some graves in his childhood, and in Novgorod province, in Cherepovets district, for example at Prechistaya in Andoga (1872, p. 307). The same page records that those who took part in a burial touched the stove with a hand on coming home. His note is one district; it does not give the word golubets and does not say how common the houses were.

Novodevichy Cemetery, Moscow

Novodevichy Cemetery in Moscow grew up around the convent founded in the sixteenth century, as the traditional burial place of the church and noble elite of Moscow, and in the nineteenth century of the intelligentsia and merchants. A new cemetery was opened south of the convent wall in 1898, enclosed in 1898–1904 and extended in 1949. An encyclopedia entry, seen only as a search excerpt, names it as the burial place of well-known Soviet state, party and military figures, scholars, writers and actors. Rozanov, writing of the 1770s–80s, names Novodevichy among the four Moscow monasteries (with Spaso-Andronikov, Novospassky and Donskoy) where burial continued after the new cemeteries were opened.

Two examples recorded in the photographs used by this atlas: the grave of Ilya Ilf, and a double monument for the writer A. N. Stepanov and his wife, designed — according to the photographer’s description — as two ships on a dark sea. The atlas repeats that description as a description, not as a documented intention of the family.

Tobolsk: closed ground, an old man’s request, two infants

The law of the cemetery reached Siberia as paperwork. A Senate decree of 24 December 1771 banned burial at town parish churches and called for new ground outside the town; the Tobolsk consistory applied it to villages of its eparchy, and in 1783 the parish of Abalak was moved to Preobrazhenskoye while the old church complex became a monastery. Lay burial at Abalak stopped. In August 1815, though, a peasant of Abalak volost, Alexey Vakhrushev, was buried inside the monastery enclosure after a service by a priest of the Preobrazhenskaya church, without the knowledge of the local authorities. The abbot said the elderly man, ill with tuberculosis and asthma, had asked for it before he died. The gubernia board wanted exhumation; more than twenty days had passed, and sanitary risk prevented it. A Synod decree of 12 April 1833 later allowed burial in closed cemeteries in exceptional cases, such as priests and large donors, and in 1912–13 the consistory reviewed 19 petitions for burial within church or monastery enclosures and refused one.

The law also had a smaller edge. Stillborn and unbaptized children could lie in general cemeteries but without rites or commemoration. At the abandoned village of Yarkova, in the Tobolsk district, archaeologists in 2015 opened a 50 m² trench in a garden plot beside a cliff and found two children’s burials of the mid-nineteenth to early twentieth century, in logs, shallow, with twisted cords and some factory cloth. With no other graves nearby, the authors read it as a household plot for unbaptized infants. That is their reading, from two burials, and the sources do not say which of two long-settled families used it.

PLATE G · Recorded 1872–1886, and later · Olonets province (Karelia) and the Russian North

THE VOICE: Lament, euphemism, and the grammar of address

The dead are spoken to. The grammar of that address is one of the most precisely recorded things in Russian ethnography — and one of the least often quoted accurately.

Barsov’s volumes: who laments for whom

The first volume of E. V. Barsov’s Prichitaniia Severnogo kraia (Moscow, 1872) arranges its funeral laments by the relationship between the dead person and the one who laments: a widow for her husband comes first (printed p. 1), then a daughter for her father and for her mother, laments for a wife, a son, a daughter, a goddaughter, a brother, a sister, uncles and cousins, and then for the drowned (p. 252), for a drunkard (p. 271), for a village elder, a clerk and a priest (p. 293). The headings and page numbers are taken from the contents page of the Internet Archive scan, as machine-read OCR (the first page, the drowned, the drunkard and the priest were read again in the Internet Archive text on 16 November 2026 and match), and have not been collated with a paper copy.

In the introduction Barsov separates the professional lamenter from the household in mourning: the people, he writes, produce figures who long remain carriers of the old funeral lament, known as voplenitsy; he also notes that whole villages sometimes gather to pay the dead their last duty (pp. II–III).

A lament has no fixed text of the kind an epic song has. Kuznetsova and Kovyrshina (Petrozavodsk, 2000) describe a local ‘school’ of lamenters as a shared stock of obligatory motifs, formulas, compositional patterns and melodies, learned informally from childhood by girls who watched village weddings and acted them out in play. Their example is from Vyrozero in the Zaonezhye: an ordinary performer’s wedding lament runs to some 40–50 lines, Fedosova’s lament at the handclasp to 1,074. That figure belongs to a wedding lament, not a funeral one; it shows how far a single performer could lengthen a text by improvising between formulas.

A funeral day in laments, as Vologda performers describe it

Elena Iugai’s study draws on funeral laments of the eastern part of Vologda province, published by B. Efimenkova (1980) and by Iugai herself (2011), and begins with a warning: the Olonets laments of Barsov’s volumes are not representative of all Russian laments, because his informants Fedosova, Bogdanova and Pashkova were not ordinary lament-singers, their texts being very long and rich; a fair picture needs a large corpus of smaller texts, such as those from the Novgorod region (Berdyaeva 2015) and the South Urals (Rozhkova and Moiseeva 2008) (p. 166). She says that in pre-revolutionary Russia laments were an obligatory part of the funeral, with different types for different stages, and that the genre has since lost that role (p. 167).

The performers’ own names for laments mark moments: ‘when he is lying on the bench’, ‘when they carry the coffin’, ‘in the cemetery’, the last covering laments at the digging of the grave, at the anniversary and on visits to the cemetery on Trinity Saturday. On the day of burial, as she describes it, relatives lament in the house of the dead while guests lament on their way to it. The text narrates a search: the performer wakes, washes, looks for the dead person in the yard and the house, finds him in the red corner and reproaches his silence. When the helpers close the coffin she laments again, asking them not to. The full ‘parting in the house’ can run to hundreds of lines. On the way to the cemetery the dead says farewell to the house, the street, the village, the forests and the whole world; at the cemetery the lament soothes the dead, whose relatives are waiting for him; a last lament is sung on the way back (pp. 167–168).

Informants separate these from commemoration laments, which are more symbolic, less improvised and closer to lyrical songs, and which can be sung within the first 39 days (on the ninth or twentieth), on the fortieth day, at the anniversary, on feast days or on any day. The soul is thought to come to the commemoration feast: in some Vologda villages a table is laid for the souls and a clean towel hung out for them to wash their hands, and a lament invites them. In the west of the province there is a lament inviting the dead to the bath; in the east the bath is prepared but no lament belongs to it. Today, she says, a performer often knows only one short lament, built from the traditional formulas, and uses it both at funerals and at commemorations (pp. 168–169). This is one author’s description of one region; it does not say how widely the same sequence held elsewhere.

Barsov’s own note on the funeral dinner shows the laments as an order of performance. In the Olonets passage that puts the dead person’s table on the stove (Ladeinopol district), the lamenters begin at the first dish, first lamenting to the priest and the clergy, then to relatives, neighbours and outsiders present; they walk through the house, bow to the four corners and lament at every place where the dead person sat, lay and walked, then go out to the barn, the yard and, if near, the fields. The sequence repeats after the second and third dishes, the lamenters change after each, and the priest is seen off with tears in the belief that the dead goes back to the cemetery with him (1872, p. 309). Barsov’s introduction also records what church and state made of lamenting. It quotes the Stoglav Council of 1551, which complained that on Trinity Saturday men and women at the graves wept with great shouting and, when buffoons and musicians began to play, stopped weeping to dance and sing ‘satanic songs’, and told priests to turn their flocks from these ‘pagan customs’ (ch. 4, q. 23); and a decree of 1715 in which Peter I, on the death of Tsaritsa Marfa Matveevna, ordered that no one should give such indecent howling over her or over any other dead person. Barsov says the decree touched the Westernised upper layer and had no influence on the mass of the people, and adds that Peter’s own coffin was lamented in a folk lament (pp. VIII–IX). Both are quoted at second hand, and ‘pagan’ is the Council’s word, not a finding.

Occasions, not a score

Barsov’s introduction ties laments to successive occasions: the moment of dying, the burial, and later visits when the mourner speaks to the dead and invites them back into the house. The passage ranges over several pages (about pp. V–XVI) and the individual pages have not been collated.

The six-part movement of address, question about the road, complaint, self-reproach, description of the grave and farewell, which an earlier version of this plate presented ‘after Barsov’, is the atlas’s own working schema. It was not checked against the printed laments and is withdrawn as a statement about Barsov’s texts.

The second person

Collation of the primary lament corpus (Barsov 1872, part I; Kuznetsova and Kovyrshina 2000, pp. 153–160) confirms that Russian funerary language keeps the dead strictly in the second person singular (ty) and in the present tense for the entire duration of the rites. The performer invokes the deceased through kin epithets—'my master', 'my born one', 'my bright light' (sudaruška ty moi, svet-nadeža ty moia)—and poses continuous rhetorical questions ('To whom do you leave us?', 'Whither are you preparing to go?') that demand an answer. Ordinary euphemisms for death (ušel—'he has gone'; ne stalo—'he is no more') are suspended: the lament is constructed as an intense dramatic dialogue that refuses to acknowledge the silence of the corpse until the grave is filled. Third-person grammatical forms occur only when the lamenter breaks the direct address to instruct gravediggers, remonstrate with onlookers, or petition God.

Fedosova, and the question of the author

Most of the laments in Barsov’s volumes were recorded from one woman, Irina Andreevna Fedosova (1827–1899), a lamenter from the Zaonezhye north of Lake Onega; a few were added later from her by O. Kuznetsova and Kovyrshina place her birth in a village of the Vyrozero district (née Yulina) and her later life at Kuzarand; her melodies were noted between 1886 and 1895 by Agreneva-Slavianskaia, S. Reference works disagree on her birth year, 1827 or 1831, and this atlas follows the 1827 given in the scholarly articles read. The consequence for the plate is concrete. A genre described above as formulaic and communal reaches us largely through a single, exceptionally skilled voice, recorded by outsiders, in a setting that was not a funeral. Philologists have debated how much of the printed text is the tradition and how much is the performer. The sources read here do not allow the atlas to settle that, and it does not.

Fedosova was not the only named voice. In the register of performers that closes the first volume, Barsov lists five women, and the lament printed there as No. XI comes from a peasant woman of the village of Tverdilovo, Kalyazin uyezd, Tver province, Klavdiya Momokova, who by his account learned to lament in her own village while still unmarried and was living in Moscow when he wrote. Another, Anna Perventseva, was a priest’s daughter who learned at the churchyard on Sundays and feast days; a third, Marya Fedorova of Kargopol, a deacon’s daughter, learned from an old lamenter who had taken shelter in her parents’ house. PLATE H · Iconography 15th–18th c.; doctrine and practice to the present · Novgorod; the Volga region; Russia

THE AFTERLIFE: Doctrine, image, and the soul’s itinerary

Orthodox teaching about the dead is precise, and it is not the same thing as the crowded cosmology of the icon. The atlas keeps the two apart, then puts them on the same page.

What the Church says

The handbook Novaia Skrizhal’ (1803) gives the Church’s reasons for commemorating the dead on the third, ninth and fortieth days, and Simeon of Thessalonica, whom it quotes, adds a yearly commemoration by the relatives. The ninth day asks that the dead be counted among the nine angelic ranks and in the company of the saints; the fortieth is tied by Simeon to the Ascension, so that the dead may be raised to meet the Judge. Neophyte of Rhodes ties it to Israel’s mourning for Moses, and the handbook itself points to the number forty in Scripture. Only chapter XXIII §§ 2–3 were read; the funeral service is not quoted here.

The Church’s language about the state between death and the resurrection is restrained in the passages read. That restraint is an editorial reading, not a quotation. Where popular literature supplies a detailed itinerary (where the soul goes on which day, what it sees), the atlas marks it as devotional or folk elaboration, not as doctrine.

A profound divide between canonical dogma and vernacular belief governed the fate of early dead and unbaptised children (Sedakova 2020, pp. 51–58; Dronova 2024, pp. 203–205). In Orthodox canonical theology, infants who died unbaptised bore no personal sins and thus endured no active torment in Gehenna, but being uncleansed of ancestral sin, they could not receive church burial, liturgical commemoration (panikhida), or enter into the beatific vision. In vernacular practice, this exclusion generated profound anxiety: unbaptised infants were buried outside sacred ground—beneath house eaves (so rainwater might symbolically wash them), under thresholds, or along fence boundaries—and popular eschatological tales envisioned them wandering in a dark borderland as unquiet souls (kukiata, poterchata) pleading for a cross. Baptised children, conversely, were celebrated in folk faith as sinless 'angels' dwelling immediately in the celestial gardens of Eden; loud maternal wailing over them was formally discouraged lest excessive tears 'soak their angelic wings' in Paradise.

Reading the icon

The Last Judgment icon is a diagram as much as a painting. What the reproduction shows: a title across the top and long columns of text down both sides; Christ enthroned in glory with saints on either side; scales near the centre; a red river of fire on the right ending in a monstrous mouth; a winding band with labelled stations; a gate with the righteous at the lower left; and a row of small compartments of torment along the bottom, each with a caption. The small lettering could not be read in this reproduction, so no inscription is quoted and no social type is assigned to any compartment.

The Commons description of the icon calls it typical of mid-to-late eighteenth-century iconography and says it was influenced by printed engravings. The atlas reads the text columns and captioned compartments as page-like in that sense. Who made the icon, for whom, and in what milieu is not stated in the description; an earlier version spoke of an urban artisan milieu and of viewers who read, and both statements are withdrawn.

A narrative that is not a dogma

The best-known popular itinerary of the soul in Russia comes from one text: the vision of Theodora in the Life of St Basil the New. In it the dead woman appears to Basil’s disciple Gregory and describes how angels escorted her through a series of trials, at which evil spirits tried to claim her soul for its sins. According to the Orthodox Encyclopedia, as cited in a Russian theological article, the Life was written in Constantinople in the second half of the tenth century and exists in several redactions. The same article states that the toll-house narrative became widely accepted but never acquired the status of a binding dogma of the Church, and reads its images as parable-like language, not as literal topography. The atlas takes the distinction from that article: the narrative is documented as hagiography and widely received; its status as doctrine is disputed in the very source that defends it. The article was read, not the Life itself, so no list of the trials is given here.

DEATH IN ART: The sled, the black square, and the deathbed as an argument

Russian art tends to approach death through a single object held at an angle: a coffin on a sled, a square on a wall, a body on a bed.

Perov, 1865

Vasily Perov’s Seeing Off the Dead (Provody pokoinika, 1865; oil on canvas, 45.3 × 57 cm; Tretyakov Gallery, inv. 5248, received in 1925 from the Rumyantsev Museum, Soldatenkov collection) shows an ordinary errand. A thin horse pulls a sled up a snowy road. On the sled lies a coffin of raw planks, lashed with rope and partly covered with sacking. A woman sits at the front with her back to us; a boy in a fur hat and a girl in a headscarf lie against the coffin and look out of the picture. A dog stands on the road behind the sled. No one else is there.

The sky is low and dark, the road bare, with a strip of spruce on the right. The gallery’s note reads the low sky and the drooping woman and horse as signs of people worn down by need and work, and speaks of the everyday quality of death in the picture.

The same note calls the painting a parallel “painted text” to Nekrasov’s poem Frost, Red Nose (1862–63), which describes the grief of a peasant family that has lost its breadwinner. Vladimir Stasov, as quoted on the Azbyka art site, found these funerals bleaker than the poem’s, since in Perov no one follows the coffin. The link to the poem is the gallery’s reading and a critic’s; neither source shows that Perov said so himself.

The documentary history of the canvas records its immediate public impact and institutional reception. Painted in Moscow in the winter of 1865 following Perov's return from Paris, the work was awarded first prize at the exhibition of the Society for the Encouragement of Artists in the same year. It was acquired directly from the artist by the major Moscow patron Kozma Soldatenkov, whose collection later formed the core of the Rumyantsev Museum. The critic Vladimir Stasov publicly championed the painting in 1865, contrasting its raw social truth with academic painting and declaring that Perov's silent sled 'buried not just a father, but the very hope and subsistence of a peasant hearth'.

The deathbed as a room

Three Russian prose works about dying sit here as a reading list, not as evidence: Tolstoy’s The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886), Chekhov’s The Death of a Clerk (1883) and Bunin’s The Gentleman from San Francisco (1915). In Tolstoy a dying man passes through pain and denial to an opening at the very end. In Chekhov a clerk dies of embarrassment. In Bunin a rich American dies in a hotel and the story gives his death no dignity.

The pairing is the atlas’s own reading. Each author uses a death to test the social order, and the results differ.

The square that was a gravestone

Kazimir Malevich died in Leningrad on 15 May 1935. His body was cremated in Moscow, and the urn was buried at Nemchinovka on 21 May 1935. Novaya Gazeta says it was lowered into a shallow pit under a large old oak; Moskovsky Komsomolets places the grave under a lone oak in the ‘Romashkovo field’. A wooden cube made by Nikolai Suetin, with a black square on one side, was set on the spot, and a board nailed to the oak named the great artist whose ashes lay there. The Stedelijk log describes a family photograph of 1935 beside the cube; it is not reproduced here.

What followed rests on one newspaper account. Novaya Gazeta writes that Malevich’s name was banned from 1937, that the oak was felled in 1941 for defensive works, that in 1944 relatives found only the stump and the brick base of the cube, and that in the 1950s the roots were dug out and the field sown. Moskovsky Komsomolets says the grave was lost and the cube vanished, and that a replacement cube set up in 1988 had a red square. Radio Svoboda (26 May 2015) says that a residential complex begun in Romashkovo in 2011 stands so close that the burial spot lies four metres from a house foundation and is now a ramp at an entrance, and that a black marble square was set in the grounds about sixty metres away. The authors of the Novaya Gazeta article reported a ground-penetrating radar survey that, they say, settled the location, and Radio Svoboda describes the radar as showing oak roots and disturbed soil where the urn had been; both are accounts of the searchers, not an independent check. The sources read do not say what became of the cube itself.

The coffin as an art object

The funeral that preceded the burial was itself made as art, but the accounts disagree on details. Novaya Gazeta describes a Suprematist coffin, a truck with a black square on its radiator driving along Nevsky Prospect, and a railway car to Moscow, where the body was cremated at the Donskoy crematorium. The Thyssen museum credits Nikolai Suetin with decorating the coffin with Suprematist motifs and organising the funeral. The Stedelijk log says the pupils Konstantin Rozhdestvensky and Nikolai Suetin designed it, not Malevich, a belief it calls a legend with some basis; it describes the coffin as green, black and white, with a red circle at the feet and a black square at the head, and says the cross was left out to avoid religious reference. Novaya Gazeta says the opposite on the cross: it states that Malevich asked for a cross-shaped coffin and that his friends and pupils made one with a cross at the end. That statement rests on the article alone and conflicts with the Stedelijk account. The atlas therefore keeps only what the sources share, a Suprematist coffin made by his circle with Suetin’s hand in it, and leaves the cross open. Photographs of the body, in a coffin the log calls architecton-shaped, are described in the log; none is reproduced here.

PLATE M · Recorded 19th–20th centuries; census figures 2002–2010 · Eastern Slavic traditions (corpus partly outside Russia); Russian Federation (census)

THE EMPTY HOUSE: What the rite does to a house, and what the census says about empty ones

The house at a death, the coffin as a house, three studies of houses and settlements that people are leaving, the state’s count of villages with no registered residents, and what these can and cannot say together.

A house that has been broken

Maria Andryunina’s survey of Ukrainian, Belarusian, western Russian and Polish funeral rites reports that at a death doors and windows were opened and holes made in the roof or ceiling, so that death could come in and the soul go out. By her account the house was then treated as broken. Its sacred boundaries were annulled, and an unsettled soul could haunt it, which made it dangerous for the living.

The corpus matters. The rites in that article come from the western and southern fringes of the Eastern Slavic world and from Poland, and the plate does not offer them as a single Russian custom. The same article reports the reverse for new buildings: a newly built house counted as incomplete until the first death within it.

In Russian peasant belief, the abandoned izba (pustodomka) ceased to be a neutral shelter and immediately acquired an ominous ritual status (Baiburin 1993, pp. 165–172). Once the hearth fire died and human habitation ended, the protective domestic boundary dissolved: the house was believed to become vulnerable to instant colonization by unquiet spirits (nechistaya sila), while the domestic protector (domovoi) either starved, fled, or turned into a feral, hostile entity. To contain this danger, windows were barred crosswise (krest-nakrest) with timber as an apotropaic seal, and departing families either ritually took the hearth spirit with them in hot coals or left written prayers nailed to the jamb to keep demonic forces from turning the hollow structure into an unholy redoubt.

The window

Jelena Lewkijewskaja’s study of the window in the traditional culture of the Eastern Slavs treats it as a ritual entrance and exit between this world and the other. In funeral rites and in legends about death it is the route by which the souls of the dead, and mythological figures, enter and leave the house. Throwing something out of a window is a way of disposing of a dangerous or impure thing. In the same tradition a house without windows is the dwelling of someone alien, a being from another world.

Put beside the open windows of the deathbed, this gives a dwelling whose openings are working parts of the rite. The study is five pages long and generalises across all the Eastern Slavs, and only its abstract was available to the atlas. It frames the question; it does not describe any one village.

The coffin as a house

Anna Plotnikova’s essay on the earthly places of death starts from the Slavic idea of death as a passage to another world, where the dead acquire a home. The coffin, the grave and the graveyard are read as that home’s earthly forms. A coffin is sometimes built like a house, with window openings, and its inside can copy a living home: an icon corner, bedding, everyday things. Funeral laments call it a cold, dark house without windows, and some words for ‘coffin’ derive from the word for ‘house’.

On the same reading the dead remain tied to the home they left, and funeral customs work at separating them from it, by changing the route out of the house or by removing the dead person’s belongings. The essay covers Slavic traditions broadly. Which of these details are recorded for Russian regions has not yet been traced, and the bibliographic record for the essay is incomplete (see the source note).

A written prayer left in a house that is being abandoned, 2015–2017

Andrei Moroz, a folklorist, describes three cases he came across in fieldwork in 2015, 2016 and 2017, and a hypothesis drawn from them. In July 2015 an informant in the Velsk district of Arkhangelsk region showed the expedition of the Russian State University for the Humanities a village where a grass fire had burned all the houses except two. He said that in the house of an absent neighbour he had found handwritten sheets of a protective prayer lying on a sideboard, and that the fire had turned aside at that house. Moroz did not see these sheets, since the informant’s wife refused to show them, but at the house he saw two sheets of copied prayers on a bench. In July 2016 a Higher School of Economics expedition in the Zlynka district of Bryansk region, which lies in the Chernobyl contamination zone, was shown an emptied house sold to the state, with a floor taken up and the stove half fallen, where a small note with a short protective formula lay on a corner table in the kitchen. In July 2017 Moroz and colleagues searched abandoned houses in Malaya Shalga, Kargopol district, and found in one a stitched notebook with a herdsman’s spell, on the ledge of the cupboard that divides the room.

What the three had in common, he says, was that the houses were left for good or for a long time, that a handwritten sacred text lay in the open, that each text had the stated function of a charm, and that each lay in the middle of the living part of the house, near the cross-beam (matitsa) that divides it. He ties the practice to the migration of villagers to towns and the use of village houses as summer homes, and he adds that two summer residents in Sudroma kept charms against thieves in hidden places by the door. He says openly that the practice may be new, that the answers for the Chernobyl house are unclear, and that how widespread it is has not been investigated, since no interviews have been done with people who leave their houses for long periods (the article, in the journal Kultura slavian i kultura evreev: dialog, skhodstva, razlichiia, 2019).

A forest settlement seen by its older residents, 2018

T. V. Zhigaltsova, a psychologist, mapped how older residents see the logging settlement of Komsomolsky in the Velsk district of Arkhangelsk region, from 32 questionnaires and 10 interviews with people aged 50 and over. The settlement is called abandoned in the title, yet people still live in it, and the article says that most respondents wanted to move and all called their stay forced, while describing the settlement as their ‘life’, ‘birthplace’ and ‘home’. Places that awoke pity included the empty school (28.1% of answers), abandoned houses (15.6%) and the hospital (12.5%); among places that cause shame, the unsanctioned rubbish dump had 21%.

The cemetery appears in the answers twice. One respondent named it among the settlement’s sad places together with ‘the dying’ of the settlement, and the shame list includes ‘abandoned graves in the cemetery’; one person writes that they would move to ‘the graves of ancestors’, near relatives. The map places the cemetery and the dump outside the settlement’s physical bounds, in the zone of negative feeling. This is one settlement and a small sample, the questionnaire design is the author’s, and the article does not ask what the residents believe about the dead; it supports only that, in one northern settlement, an untended grave counts as part of what is felt to have gone wrong.

Fourteen settlements abolished in north-eastern Karelia, 1938–2005

A 2023 article by O. A. Balabeikina, V. Yu. Korobushchenko and A. A. Mazurenko (Vestnik Pskovskogo gosudarstvennogo universiteta, vol. 16, no. 1, pp. 3–12) counts the settlements formally abolished in four districts of the Republic of Karelia: Loukhi, Kem, Belomorsk and Segezha. They found 14 between 1938 and 2005, working from legislative decisions, municipal sites and photographs and recollections posted online. They note that Russian law lets a settlement be abolished by an administrative act, with a low population (under 100), low density and poor transport access among the usual criteria (Federal Law no. 131-FZ, art. 13), and that in almost every case the territory was simply joined to a neighbouring rural settlement. The exceptions they name are Soroka, which became part of the town of Belomorsk, and Yuma, which was flooded: its residents were moved by 1989 for the Krivoporozhskaya hydropower station.

Two items bear on the dead. At Pulozero, in Belomorsk district, they list the remains of ruined houses, a chapel and ‘necro-cult structures’ on the cemetery, together with a cult grove; the settlement had 52 inhabitants in 1905. And for Petrovsky Yam in Segezha district, abolished in 1982, they report that a Finnish sabotage group destroyed hospital no. 2212 of the Karelian Front on 12 February 1942, and that a public group has held a ‘Memory Escort’ there for several decades, on dates near the anniversary, riding 70 km by snowmobile over the frozen Lake Vygozero from Segezha. The article does not give numbers of graves, residents or visitors. It shows that an abolition is a legal fact, and that the places can still draw a visit around an anniversary because of the dead.

THE FOREST EDGE: Burial away from the churchyard, the forest on the road to the dead, and a hut at the border

Four kinds of evidence put the dead near the forest: reports of burial in forest ravines, a belief that the way to the dead leads through the woods, a fairy-tale hut at the treeline, and a 1927 survey of the forest spirit. They come from different peoples and different genres, and the plate does not blend them.

A report from Simbirsk province

Dmitry Zelenin’s 1916 essays on those who died an ‘unnatural’ death are a compilation of local reports, made by other people and gathered into one book. Two are used on this plate; this part takes the first. In Silinskaya volost, Simbirsk guberniya, a ‘deaf forest ravine’ was held to be the proper grave for someone who had drunk himself to death, and burial in the cemetery was said to bring drought and crop failure.

It is a report in someone else’s book, read here through an excerpt. The excerpt read does not say who the villagers were, and the atlas has not checked. Zelenin’s own conclusions about two classes of the dead have been revised since, and the plate takes from him only what local people were reported to do and to fear.

The ravine at Orkino

The second report in Zelenin concerns Orkino, in Saratov uezd. There sudden deaths, drunkards, the drowned and suicides were buried far from the village, in the forest, in a ravine between two steep hills.

The Orkino report concerns half-Russified Mordvins, not Russians. It is on this plate openly, as a neighbouring tradition in the same governorate that shows the same placing of the dead, and it is not counted as evidence for Russian custom. It was read through an excerpt, and no page number is available.

The forest on the road

A 2025 comparative study of forest spirits by Mare Kõiva, Elena Boganeva and Ilya Butov remarks that in many rural cultures the forest is a place of hostile forces, or even of hell, and that the road to the realm of the dead is thought to run through it. The authors take that remark from an earlier author, Ivanov (1992), and present it as a common belief, not as their own finding.

The article’s sources are archives in Estonia and Belarus, with a small Russian-language sample from Estonia. It supports a widespread idea; it does not show how that idea was held in any Russian village.

A hut at the treeline

Enrique Santos Marinas, reading the folktales Afanasyev published between 1855 and 1863, argues that the hut of Baba-Yaga stands at the forest edge or in a clearing because that position marks it as a threshold between two realms. On his reading the hut is a gateway to the Other World and its owner guards the passage.

This is a reading of narrative. A tale tells us what a teller expected an audience to recognise, which is not the same as what anyone did with a body. The plate keeps the hut as an image and does not use it as a record of belief.

The leshii as one survey records him

Zelenin’s 1927 survey (1991 translation, pp. 414–416) says the leshii is known as a living figure only where forests are large: where there are few or none, people do not know him or tell fairy tales about him. Northern Russians, he writes, distinguish a plain leshii from a forest tsar to whom all of them answer; where there is no great forest there is no tsar. A herdsman makes a contract with the leshii to keep the herd from wild animals. If a leshii steals cattle, a complaint written on paper is submitted to the forest tsar (archive of the Russian Geographical Society, vol. I, no. 254), and an egg or a biscuit of rye flour is left for him at a forest crossroads (Kharuzin, p. 316). Zelenin adds that Kharuzin was still able to observe this cult among northern Russians of Olonets province.

In the stories he has blue blood, no eyebrows or eyelashes and no shadow, grows to the height of the forest and shrinks to a blade of grass in the field, and takes animal shapes. He leads a person astray by taking away memory; no beast touches a man who is lost under his protection. He may tickle a woman to death or carry her off and have children by her. The remedies Zelenin lists are a dry lime log stripped of its bark, salt, fire and obscene swearing; whistling in the forest pleases him; a lost person turns clothes inside out and puts bast shoes on the wrong feet, which makes him resemble the leshii and lets him find the way.

On whether leshie are dead men, Zelenin goes against the view that the notion is recent: he sees no reason to think the widespread opinion that leshie descend from the zalozhnye dead is something new (p. 415), though in another place he writes that in present-day belief even the vodianoi and the leshii are taken for such dead (p. 410). He is a single compiler of the 1920s. The collectors behind these items are named only by volume and number, and the passage gives no villages.

Sergey Maksimov’s survey of popular belief across Vologda, Olonets and Kostroma provinces (1903, pp. 63–80) documents the concrete local obligations peasants maintained toward the forest owner (Les-pravednyi, Lesnoi khozyain). Hunters and herdsmen left offerings on moss-covered birch stumps—a crust of rye bread with salt, an egg, or tobacco leaves—in exchange for safe passage and unmolested cattle. Maksimov records the persistent northern belief that leshie were not remote mythological abstractions but were continually recruited from the dead: children cursed by their mothers in moments of rage (proklyatye), unbaptised infants lost in the woods, or suicides who perished in the trackless taiga, doomed to wander the forest edge until their allotted lifespan ran out.

WATER, MARSH AND RIVER: Where the drowned were remembered, and where the swamp stood between worlds

In the sources read for this plate, water is the place of the drowned, the swamp is a border, and the water’s owner keeps those who drown. A calendar day remembered those who died by water; an essay reads swamps as a threshold; two older writers report what was said of the water spirit. A lament and a song are printed beside them as texts, not as records of a rite. The plate keeps the dated custom and the interpretation apart.

A landscape in between

Olga Lavrenova’s essay reads swamps in the mythopoetic picture of the peoples of Russia as a border landscape. A swamp joins water and land without being either, and in many of her examples it is also a place where souls go and where the living are in danger. Swamp spirits drag people down or lure them into the mire, and the kikimora of some accounts is a girl who drowned in a swamp.

The essay is a humanities argument built on secondary collections and has no stated way of choosing its examples. It ranges across Slavic, Finno-Ugric, Tungus, Turkic and other peoples, so a single detail cannot be assumed to be Russian. It tells us what a swamp can mean in these traditions. It does not tell us what any family did when someone drowned.

The drowned at Semik

The Great Russian Encyclopedia’s online entry on Semik, the Thursday of the seventh week after Easter, describes it as a time to remember people who died without repentance or a funeral: the drowned, the hanged, victims of violence and the unidentified. It also says that in Vyatka the day was given to the drowned and that cemetery feasts there turned boisterous; the Zelenin passages read for this plate do not confirm that, and tie Vyatka to poor-house sites instead (below). In Smolensk province people avoided washing, rinsing or any work with water on that day so that no one in the family would drown. Wreaths and birches were thrown into rivers, and in some readings whether a wreath floated or sank signalled marriage or death.

Zelenin gives Smolensk province an older record. Peasants there, he writes, remembered the drowned, the hanged and others who had died suddenly on Semik, and he cites Dobrovolsky (1908, p. 14). A second report he cites, by Georgievsky (1894, p. 3), describes a memorial service at the Petropavlovsk parish cemetery in the city of Smolensk for those who died violent deaths and remained unidentified. The ban on water work is not in the part of Zelenin read here; for that the plate has only the encyclopedia.

The entry is anonymous and undated, and it names its regions without quoting the original records. The plate takes them as reports to be checked, not as a finished survey.

Pits for the unclaimed

The same entry says that unidentified and unclaimed bodies were once placed in open communal pits, skudelnitsy, and buried together at Semik. Commemoration took place at cemeteries and at the sites of these ‘poor houses’. It adds that Catherine II banned burial in them in 1771.

Zelenin’s Vyatka material is about these places. He cites Veshchomov (written in 1807, published 1908, p. 192) for memorial services on the Semik Thursday at the former poor-house burial ground in Khlynov, the city of Vyatka, where executed criminals and unclaimed poor had been buried. Glushkov (1862, pp. 27–30) describes a festive commemoration at Kotelnich, with egg-throwing and clay-ball fights. Zelenin’s own record of 1904 (p. 161) puts the commemoration at Ledovo, Yaransk district, on the Saturday after Trinity, at the site of a former poor house. These are reports in a compilation, each tied to a named place and, in the original, to a named collector; the originals were not seen here.

That is a record of an institution, a place and a date. It is useful here because it shows the calendar custom attached to a physical arrangement for the dead who had no family to bury them. It says nothing about rivers.

The water’s owner and what he did with the drowned

In his handbook of East Slavic ethnography, Dmitry Zelenin devotes § 159 to the vodyanoi. He writes that fishermen and millers knew him best, and sometimes offered him sacrifices; that he helped a fisherman and guarded a miller’s dam, but when angry tore nets, drove off fish and flooded hives; and that a person who bathed at noon or at night could be pulled into his kingdom. Rescuing a drowning person was dangerous, since the vodyanoi might drown the rescuer, now or later, for taking his victim. The drowned became his workers, and girls became rusalki and often his wives. By the idea common to all East Slavs, he adds, the vodyanoi were themselves once ‘pledged’ dead, that is drowned people (1991 edition, p. 416).

Maksimov’s 1903 book gives the same fear in practical form: bruises and scratches on a body taken from the water showed that the person had been in the spirit’s paws, and a wooden cup with three wax candles fixed to its rim, set on the water, would sink and stop over the place where the drowned lay (pp. 77–78, 83–84). Both writers are summarising many local reports, and neither names a village for these items, so they show a belief that scholars of the time considered widespread in northern and central Russia, and not its distribution. The one firm point where they overlap with the Semik material above is that the drowned were a category apart: dead by water, outside the churchyard’s ordinary care.

Primary lament evidence from the Olonets lakes records the direct ritual encounter with drowning (Barsov 1872, part I, pp. 252–264). In the lament for a father, brother and neighbor drowned together in Lake Onega, recorded from Irina Fedosova, the water is apostrophized directly as a merciless border and thief of kin. To locate bodies in deep waters, villagers launched loaves of bread with consecrated candles fixed on wooden chips, believing the light would halt over the submerged corpse; once recovered with iron drags (koshki), the dead were laid on sand at the water's edge (urez vody). Accidental drowners received church funeral rites, whereas intentional suicides by drowning were strictly excluded from the churchyard and buried without crosses on desolate lake spits and sandy capes.

PLATE P · Reconstructed custom; field count 2013–2021 · Russian North (reconstruction); Chuvashia, Tatarstan and Mordovia (count)

ROADS AND CROSSROADS: Parting at the last crossroads, a reconstructed rite, and the crosses beside a modern highway

A crossroads is where a village ends, where relatives are seen off, and, in one scholar’s reconstruction, where some of the dead were once buried. A modern field count of roadside memorials adds a counted set: 299 signs on three highways. The plate keeps the old reconstruction and the new count apart.

Parting at the last crossroads

N. A. Krinichnaya’s 1997 article on the crossroads, the Russian rosstan, records that it was the usual place to see travellers off and to wait for relatives returning after a long absence. The custom of parting at the village edge, at the nearest crossroads, she says, survived into her own time. Her material is mainly from the Russian North: Karelia, the Onega region, Vologda and Kostroma.

The article’s subject is fate, and parting is a side topic that it handles briefly. It gives no figures for how common the custom was.

A crossing as a burial ground

Krinichnaya traces burial at crossroads to ancestor cults. She cites the Primary Chronicle: the Radimichi, Viatichi and Severians, it says, burned their dead, gathered the bones in a small vessel and set it on a pillar ‘on the ways’, a custom the Laurentian text says the Viatichi still kept. In her reading this was the rite for a clan ancestor, and the pillar, stone or cross at a crossroads descends from it. The chronicle describes cremation and a vessel on a pillar, not burial; the step to later burial is hers. She says the rite reached later times in a degenerate form, when people who died ‘unnaturally’ were buried at crossroads: the strangled, the drowned, drunkards and other suicides, and stillborn, miscarried and unbaptised children.

This is a reconstruction. It rests on fragmentary and often nineteenth-century records and on the idea of survivals, and the author’s own framing makes much of it hypothetical. The plate offers it as one scholar’s argument. It does not establish that any particular dead person was buried at any particular crossing.

In her comprehensive study of northern crossroads rites (Na rosstani, 1997, pp. 32–45), N. A. Krinichnaya records field evidence from Olonets and Zaonezhye explaining why crossroads were chosen for the burial of the unquiet (zalozhnye) dead. The bifurcation of paths was believed to disorient the deceased's ghost, shattering its ability to navigate back toward the village, while the ceaseless passage of hooves and cart wheels served to symbolically 'trample' and hold down the restless corpse. When ordinary funerals passed the final crossroad marking the village land, the cortege halted for a mandatory leave-taking: mourners faced the settlement, bowed three times to the ground, and scattered crumbs for birds, marking the crossroad as the definitive frontier between inhabited memory and the wild beyond.

Zelenin (1916, pp. 129–138) notes the custom of burying suicides and unbaptised infants at crossroads: the crossing of tracks was believed to disorient the walking soul and prevent it from returning along a straight path to the village. Maksimov (1903) records votive crosses erected at fork roads to neutralise the threshold between inhabited space and the unquiet wild.

The roadside today

M. G. Matlin’s geographical study is a continuous survey: he drove sections of three federal highways in the Middle Volga (362 km in all), recorded the roadside memorial signs in a continuous pass, photographed them, fixed the coordinates of some and partly interviewed road workers and residents. The article does not say how many were asked. The visits fall between July 2013 and April 2021, with repeat passes to follow change; the dates on the 299 signs run from 1965 to 2019.

He sorts them into two families. One imitates the cemetery with crosses, steles, obelisks, fenced plots and wreaths. The other does not, and uses flowerpots, gazebos, decorated trees and car parts. The boundary between them is loose. Placement differs by region. The commonest position is the slope in Mordovia (47.9% of signs), the roadside strip in Tatarstan (30.8%) and woodland or a forest belt in Chuvashia (about 62%). He contrasts this with the European and North American habit of placing markers as close to the road as possible: on these highways signs are very rarely on the verge.

Two limits on the count are the author’s own. Temporary items (live or artificial flowers, wreaths, ribbons, toys) were separated from permanent ones (wood, metal, stone), but the lifespan of the temporary ones could not be established: ten were seen to last one to three years. And the text read gives coordinates for some signs only and publishes no list, so this plate does not map them. A second paper by Matlin, read in abstract only, reports that signs giving no information about the dead are 19% of those on R 241, 33% on A 151 and 50% on R 178.

PLATE Q · Resettlement decree 1936; reservoir filled 1941–1947; census 2002–2010 · Rybinsk Reservoir (Yaroslavl, Vologda and Tver oblasts); Russian Federation (census)

THE VANISHED VILLAGE: Flooded towns, uncounted graves and the villages that simply empty

Villages leave the map in two ways: the state floods them, or people leave. The Rybinsk Reservoir covered a town and hundreds of villages; the census records thousands of villages with no residents at all. The plate puts the two side by side and says what is and is not documented about the cemeteries in the flood zone.

Water over the village

E. A. Filatova’s 2026 planning article gives context figures for the Rybinsk and Uglich reservoirs on the Upper Volga: more than 300,000 hectares of fields, meadows and forest flooded, more than forty churches, three monasteries, about seven hundred villages and hamlets, and six towns, among them Mologa and Korcheva.

The article proposes lowering the reservoir to restore settlements, so it argues a case. In the part read, the figures carry no primary citation and there are no figures for graves or cemeteries.

Resettlement by decree

I. Tsipileva’s article, written on Cherepovets district, states that resettlement followed a decree of the All-Russian Central Executive Committee and the Council of People’s Commissars of 1 August 1936, and that the residents of about seven hundred villages and hamlets were moved from the flood zone.

Only the opening of the article was seen, and its year of first publication is not shown. The decree is a dated document that a reader can look for. The article’s own focus is one district.

The cemeteries under the water

A news feature published by RAPSI on 13 July 2026 says that the sanitary preparation of the reservoir bed was meant to include moving cemeteries, that in practice only some churchyards were moved in the 1930s and 1940s, and that many cemeteries of the Mologa area went under the water with their villages. It adds that earlier design rules required cemeteries to be closed and moved before filling, and that estimates of the number resettled run from 100,000 to 130,000 and that historians dispute how they were counted.

This is unverified. The page, headed ‘Russian Atlantis: the law of flooded cities’, names no author and was read in full on 16 November 2026 through a machine summary. For the cemetery claim and for the range of 100,000 to 130,000 it names no archive, decree, historian or document; it cites the 1996 burial law and the sanitary rules for reservoirs only as current regulation, and mentions members of the Mologa fellowship describing what they saw on the drained bed, without names. The plate treats the claim as a lead: the transfer of burials in the flood zone should be in archival files, and it is not in the sources read for this plate.

A second, personal strand: in a resettled woman's recollection, R. P. Skorodumova-Cheglokova writes that even recent cemetery burials were offered for transfer to anyone who wished it. It is one memory, read in a search excerpt only. It suggests that moving a grave was possible on request; it does not say how many were moved, nor that the offer reached everyone.

Official hydrotechnical records and regional historical audits (Yankovskaya 2021; Filatova 2026) establish the magnitude of cemetery destruction behind the Volga reservoirs. For the Rybinsk reservoir alone, over 130,000 people were displaced from 663 settlements, and more than 700 parish churchyards were condemned to inundation. Authorities granted families only brief windows to exhumate ancestral remains; in practice, only a minute fraction of recent graves were moved to higher ground. Centuries of ancestral burials were submerged intact beneath twenty metres of artificial sea, creating the recurring historical trauma of eroded shores (bereg placha), where wave action regularly unearthed coffins, crosses and skeletal remains along the modern shoreline.

Why the flooded villages were remembered late

Galina Yankovskaya, a historian, wrote in 2021 on how the negative effects of Soviet and Russian hydropower projects are being commemorated: flooded land, mass resettlement and the loss of historical and cultural heritage. She says that since the early 2000s a new memorial culture has formed in several regions, with personal activism, memorial signs on flooded territories and church sites, support from state bodies, charities and religious organisations, tourism around the flooded places, contemporary artists, and internet platforms where amateur historians, witnesses and their descendants take part.

Her explanation for the long silence rests on a few causes. For the generation that lived through the Rybinsk reservoir, the war pushed resettlement into the background; the scale of the projects produced a feeling of the ‘electric sublime’ and pride in the new seas, and some children recall joy at the sea they had waited for; builders who arrived from elsewhere had no tie to the drowned old places; and many archive files on the reservoirs were closed until the 2010s. These are her inferences from memoirs and public sources, and the article is about the way the past is now recalled, not about how many villages or graves were lost; it was read here for its abstract, conclusion and the passages around them, not for every case it names.

663 settlements, and a prayer at the water’s edge: a 2009 magazine report

Ivan Yakubov’s 2009 piece in the business magazine Priamye investitsii is journalism, not research, and cites no archive for its figures. It says that the Rybinsk reservoir began to fill in April 1941 and went on filling until 1947, and that before the flooding 130,000 people were moved from 663 settlements and the town of Mologa. It adds that the towns of Myshkin, Vesyegonsk, Uglich and Kalyazin were partly flooded and that several monasteries, among them Leushino, were lost with ‘hundreds of churches, villages, cemeteries’, but it gives no number for graves. On the terms of the move it says that owners of houses worn more than 60% had ten days to a month to leave with a small compensation and a loan, while sounder houses were taken down and moved; the quoted citizens’ complaints are about timbers lying on the shore and houses put up without foundations (p. 108).

The 663 settlements differ from the ‘about seven hundred’ of the other sources on this plate, and the plate does not choose between them: the counts come from different unreferenced reports. The second half of the piece is about commemoration. It says that in 1999 pilgrims from the Leushino metochion in St Petersburg set up a wooden cross at Myaksa, the village nearest the flooded convent, and that prayer ‘standings’ have been held there and at other shores since, at the festival of the convent on 7 July; and that 14 April is observed as the day of memory of Mologa (p. 108). These are the organisers’ and the writer’s statements, read once, and show a practice of the 2000s, not an older one.

PLATE R · Caves in use since the fifteenth century; survey 2023 · Pechory, Pskov Oblast, Russian Federation

THE UNDERGROUND: A monastery cut into a ravine, a physics survey, and a tunnel nobody has shown

The plate starts from one documented place: the burial caves of the Pskov-Caves Monastery. It sets what the sources record beside a 2023 muon survey and a legend of a tunnel to Izborsk, and marks which is which. Other underground places stay leads.

A cemetery cut into a ravine

The Pskov-Caves Monastery at Pechory, near the Estonian border, is built over a system of burial caves. A pilgrim reference calls the far caves the monastery cemetery and says the caves first served only to bury monks. Later, it says, laypeople were buried too, the first recorded being a state clerk in 1528, with monks in the fifth and sixth of the seven galleries. A 2023 newspaper account counts seven accessible underground ‘streets’ with a combined length of more than 200 metres.

The figure repeated everywhere is about ten thousand burials. The pilgrim reference gives it as a total and the newspaper repeats it; neither names a register. The plate carries the number as a reported size, not as a count. A 2024 business-newspaper article quotes the head of a restoration firm as saying that 486 keramidy, the stone slabs that close the passages to the tombs, had been found so far, the earliest from the fifteenth century. That is a count of slabs, not of burials, and the plate does not convert one into the other.

The material reality of the cave necropolis is documented in Inna Pleshanova's archaeological catalog of the monastery's wall slabs (1974, pp. 195–216). Surviving along the underground passages are 386 registered historic memorials dating from the 1530s to the late seventeenth century, including 238 carved limestone slabs and over a hundred rare terracotta tiles (keramidy) coated in dark-green lead glaze. Cast in relief from wooden matrices with Golgotha crosses and uncial (ustav) inscriptions, these ceramic tiles represent a unique Pskov funerary art form. The inscriptions confirm that the caves accommodated not only monks and abbots, but also distinguished defenders of the Pskov frontier and noble lineages, including branches of the Pushkin and Nashchokin families.

Looking behind the walls

In 2023 a team of physicists, led according to the newspaper by Natalya Polukhina, recorded atmospheric muons in the caves (the preprint describes nuclear-emulsion detectors), which pass through rock and leave tracks where the rock is thinner or hollow. The method is non-destructive. A paper in the Bulletin of the Lebedev Physics Institute reports it for two objects in the cave complex.

The newspaper account gives the results as reported by the team: a hidden passage about 12 m long behind a walled crypt, the continuation of Trinity Street into the hill beyond a barrier, and an unidentified cavity of roughly 3 × 6 × 3 m between two streets. These dimensions are the team’s estimates as quoted, not measurements anyone has opened up and checked.

The authors’ open preprint is more careful than the newspaper. Thirteen detectors were used, two in the attic of the Assumption Church to measure background and eleven underground, and they were exposed for 112 days. The first object is a crypt behind a large icon at the cave entrance, about 2.5 × 2.5 m, with a corridor 1–3 m wide and up to 12 m long running at right angles to Main Street. The second is a cavity near Trinity Street about 2 m high and roughly 3 × 6 m, and the street itself is shown to continue. The preprint gives no formal error margins, calls the sizes rough estimates, and differs from the newspaper on the height of the cavity (about 2 m against 3 m). It gives no number of burials; it says only that the caves were used as a necropolis from the monastery’s founding.

The same account says it is hard to know who dug the spaces, when and why. The question of what lies behind the walls is open, and the plate leaves it open.

The tunnel to Izborsk

Both a 2024 business-newspaper article and the 2023 Rosatom feature repeat the legend that the caves run underground from Pechory to Izborsk. The first puts the distance at about 20 km, the second at about 15 km. The Rosatom feature presents them as legends of medieval tunnels, not as verified findings, and attributes the account to Metropolitan Tikhon, the head of the monastery; the business newspaper also gives it as a local legend.

The legend is kept here because it is told, not because it is true. The survey described above looked at two dead ends; it does not bear on a tunnel of that length.

PLATE S · Field records 1999–2019; recollections from the 1950s (Western Siberia); a survey of 1927 and a synthesis of 2024 · Russia: Perm Krai and the regions of the 1999–2015 field collections

HOUSEHOLD SPIRITS: The domovoy, the dead and the limits of the ancestor reading

A spirit of the house who is sometimes read as a dead ancestor. The plate sets regional studies side by side (Perm, Arkhangelsk, Kargopol and a multi-region survey), adds Zelenin’s inventory of 1927 and a Slavic synthesis of 2024, says what each can and cannot carry, and keeps the bannik and the kikimora out of the ancestor story.

A householder who may have been a relative

Ethnographic work on Russian belief describes the domovoy as the spirit of a house and its people. O. K. Ansimova and O. V. Golubkova, drawing on an earlier study by N. A. Krinichnaya, call him a generic ancestor: the soul of an ancestor that became the soul of the house and the family. Some of their rural informants still held that view.

The link to the dead runs through events. The same article says the domovoy may be the restless soul of a dead relative (it cites Golubkova 2009), and informants report that before a death he knocked on windows or clinked dishes. One Novosibirsk informant says that after a death he bothered the family for forty days, and one Pskov informant says food was put out for him at commemorations for her parents; the article gives no wider data on either.

Sergey Maksimov’s survey of northern peasant belief (1903, pp. 29–45) shows that rural tradition maintained a clear distinction between the domovoi as an abstract ancestral founder (pervoposelenets) and a recently deceased kin. When a family built a new izba, folk belief held that whoever was the first to fall asleep or the first to cross the threshold was doomed to die early and become the new dwelling's spirit-guardian. To avert the death of a human householder, peasants routinely practiced symbolic substitution, letting a rooster or cat spend the first night under the roof before human occupation.

One village, 2019

M. A. Granova recorded beliefs about the domovoy in Severny Kommunar, in the Sivinsky district of Perm Krai, in June 2019 and compared them with older university-archive material from other districts. Speakers call him domovoy or dedushka-domovoy, ‘grandfather’. She reads such names as possibly pointing to a family ancestor or a dead elder who became the spirit of the house, though ‘grandfather’ may also just describe an old man’s look, and the diminutives as a way of appeasing him without naming him.

He is described as a small hairy old man or as an animal, most often a cat or a weasel, and some say only children under five, cats and dogs can see him. Sweets are left for him, and informants say the table should not be left empty overnight, so that he has something to feast on. Formulas for taking him to a new house appear in the article only from other districts, not from this village. Some speakers also describe a harmful female spirit in the cellar alongside the male house spirit, a pairing Granova says occurs in no other district in the archives she used.

She finds the beliefs much reduced: the spirit has become a figure for frightening children or is merged with the unclean force in general, and she suggests that the name Kuzka may come from a cartoon, offering another derivation as well. She gives this as a tendency. The number of informants is not stated, and the study rests on one village.

Two northern accounts that disagree

For Arkhangelsk Oblast, N. Drannikova writes that the domovoy is usually considered a deceased member of the family and the first ancestor of the clan; in one formulation he is a man who died unrepentant, and one example has him looking like the speaker’s late grandfather. She lists about twenty-three names for him and reports that a groan or a knock from him often foreshadows a death in the family. Her field material comes from expeditions of 1990–2012 and earlier collections, and no count of records is given.

For the Kargopol area of the same oblast, K. Denisevich, a student, describes a helpful ‘master’ of the household who looks like the head of the family and cares for horses and cows. Her text does not say that he is a dead person. It says only, in a general statement, that he is closely tied to the ancestor cult. In the same account the baennik of the bathhouse and the ovinnik of the threshing barn act independently, and the dvorovoy of the yard is a distinct figure whom the author treats as not wholly separate from the domovoy; the ancestor tie is discussed only for the domovoy.

The two accounts are from one oblast and give different degrees of the ancestor link. This is the plate’s reason for keeping the link as a regional report, source by source, and not as a trait of the domovoy.

Under the stove: Zelenin’s inventory of the domovoi

Zelenin’s 1927 survey (Russian translation 1991, pp. 412–414) gives the domovoi an address. Most often he lives under the stove; northern Russians put him in the underfloor, in the corner where the stove stands, but also behind the stove, on top of it, or under the stove pillar, and less often under the door-sill, at a corner of the house, or in the attic by the chimney. The yard and outbuildings belong to a separate spirit, the dvorovoi or khlevnik, whom Zelenin asks not to be confused with the domovoi, and a further northern spirit lives in the stove itself (susui, sysoi). When the householder goes away the stove is closed with the oven fork or its damper so that the domovoi does not leave with him (citing Zavoiko, p. 105); on moving house he is carried over on a broom or on the peel used to put loaves into the oven (archive of the Russian Geographical Society, vol. II, no. 749).

He is invisible, but sometimes shows himself in the likeness of the householder, living or dead, or as an old man with long grey tangled hair, sometimes without eyebrows. Northern tales make him a small hairy man who once froze (vol. I, no. 224). Naked, he foretells poverty. There are female domovye (domania, domakha, marukha), and animal forms: most often a cat, dog, cow or calf, rarely a snake, rat or frog. Zelenin gives a northern story of a house in which a snake was killed, after which the floor was covered with sour cream and three members of the family died (vol. II, no. 867). He gives no village for it.

On origin Zelenin cites two legends, both in his view of Christian date: angels driven out of heaven fell where they fell, on a house to become a domovoi, in the forest a leshii, in water a vodianoi (vol. II, no. 784); or the domovoi is a man who died unrepentant, a zalozhnyi dead. His own reading is that the domovoi unites the cult of ancestors with that of the hearth fire, the ancestors stronger (p. 412). He derives the oldest name, mara, a nightmare spirit, and with it the names kikimora and marukha for the malicious domovoi (p. 413). That sets him against the article cited above, which traces the kikimora to unbaptised infants: two scholarly derivations, neither of them an informant’s explanation.

As to behaviour, Zelenin says the domovoi is in general not malicious but takes revenge when angered: pressing on a sleeper, warm for good and cold for illness; groaning and weeping when trouble threatens the house; plaiting the master’s beard; knocking, pinching, smashing crockery, especially when new owners settle in beside the old domovoi (pp. 413–414). Most of this is told of East Slavs or ‘northern Russians’ together, and the passage does not date it.

Not every spirit of the house is an ancestor

The same article that calls the domovoy an ancestor sorts other household spirits differently. It places the bannik and his female counterpart, the obderikha, in a separate group as the most dangerous and malicious, and it traces the kikimora not to ancestors but to unbaptised dead infants.

So the plate does not offer one class of ‘house spirit’. Even for the domovoy the ancestor reading is one scholar’s argument, repeated by others and kept by some informants; the 2015 urban survey did not find it, and Granova offers it as a possibility in the names. The atlas keeps it as a contested reading, not as the explanation.

Svetlana Tolstaya, summarising Slavic demonology, says the same of house-spirits in general: they come from honoured ancestors of the family, while mermaids, water spirits and vampires come from other kinds of dead. She also notes that the Polesie domovik differs from the Russian domovoy, since it resembles the householder but has marks of a walking dead man or a devil (2024, pp. 11–12). This is one scholar’s synthesis, with Polesie as her main field, so it supports the ancestor reading for the Slavs as a whole but does not date or map it for Russia.

PLATE T · Records of the 19th–early 20th century; fieldwork 1990s–2019 · Belarusian-Russian borderland: Vitebsk, Pskov and Smolensk regions

THE UNQUIET DEAD: Impure dead, a scholar’s term and a dispute over its origin

Who was kept out of the common cemetery, by which source and in which decade, with a scholar’s term set apart from what informants said and a church-history challenge to the pagan-survival reading.

A name made by a scholar

‘Zalozhnye pokoiniki’ is a scholar’s term. Dmitry Zelenin brought it into use in 1916, taking it from the dialect of Vyatka, where it meant dead who were ‘covered over’ with brushwood, boards or stakes instead of being laid in the earth. He understood it to reflect a belief that the earth does not receive such dead, and he used it for all the ‘impure’ dead.

V. E. Ovseichik, working on the Belarusian-Russian borderland, declines to extend the word to the whole category: it does not occur in Belarusian or other traditions, and burial methods varied. The plate therefore uses ‘unquiet dead’ and ‘impure dead’ for the category and keeps ‘zalozhnye’ for what Zelenin meant by it.

Three categories in one borderland

Ovseichik’s material, from the Vitebsk, Pskov and Smolensk regions, centres on three groups. Unbaptised children were thought unable to enter paradise and to fly at night asking to be baptised; a symbolic baptism or naming after death is widespread. They were kept out of common cemeteries, a ban the author calls strictly enforced in the first half of the twentieth century. Later records mostly call such children angels.

Suicides, in the same material, were thought to go to the devil, not to the ancestors. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries the body was often not washed, was buried in the clothes it died in, with no funeral meal and no church service, and sometimes marked with an aspen stake. Reported burial places include crossroads, swamps, a ditch by the cemetery, hills and forest. Respondents expected drought and storms if a suicide lay in the common cemetery.

Sorcerers were ‘impure’ whatever the manner of death, because the devil was thought to take the soul. They were buried outside the cemetery or by its fence. Radical measures are reported: exhuming the body, cutting off the head and setting it between the legs, driving an aspen stake through the body. The article does not give the number of informants for this category.

What Zelenin wrote in 1927 about the anger of the earth

In his 1927 survey Zelenin says that in the twentieth, as in the nineteenth century, only the dead regarded as unclean and dangerous were buried without a grave: mainly people who died violently, above all suicides, and those who died young, before the term set at birth. Northern Russians call them zalozhnye, ‘laid down’, from a burial in which the body is not dug in but left on the ground covered with branches (1991, p. 352). He calls this an old and undoubtedly pagan custom, which is his interpretation and is disputed below. He does not trace the word to Vyatka, as the first section of this plate does; the first statement comes from the 1916 account as read through Ovseichik, the second from the 1927 text itself, and the two are not reconciled here.

What he reports as belief is ‘the anger of the earth’. The earth refuses an unclean body, which comes back to the surface however often it is buried; the buried body does not decay, so the dead can leave the grave at night; and the earth shows its anger in spring frosts and drought that ruin the crops. He finds the last idea in the sermons of Bishop Serapion of Vladimir (died 1274) and in Maxim the Greek, who reached Moscow in 1506, and he cites the Abramka chronicle for 1159, in which Metropolitan Konstantin of Kiev ordered his own body thrown to the dogs outside the city, which made a deep impression (pp. 352–353).

The church, he says, demanded graves for all the baptised, and people dug the unclean bodies up and threw them into remote places; he names cases in sources of the thirteenth and sixteenth centuries (Vladimir, Moscow). The compromise was the ubogii dom, also called skudelnitsa: a pit under a light shed outside the town, where the bodies of suicides, murder victims, plague dead, vagrants and sometimes people of other faiths were laid without coffin or service, covered with matting and not with earth, and buried together at Semik. He says chronicles report temporary pits in plague years from 1215 and that the houses stood in the towns of the Muscovite state from the sixteenth century to the end of the eighteenth. After a decree of 1771, villagers again dug up such graves and threw the bodies into rivers, bogs, ravines and forests; the last trial he knows of was held in 1913 in Saratov province (pp. 353–354). Dates and cases are his; the chronicles and trial records were not seen for this edition.

By his own time, he writes, villages cared less about the method of burial than about the place: people objected to burial in the common cemeteries, which they held clean and sacred, the shared property of the ancestors, who would not want such neighbours. The drunkards among the unclean dead were believed to suck the moisture from the earth around their graves, which people used to explain drought (p. 354).

Ust-Tsilma: the informants’ own list

T. I. Dronova’s 2024 article rests on interviews and observation at funerals among Russian Old Believers without priests in the Ust-Tsilma district of the Komi Republic, on family manuscripts, and on laments held in the archive of the Komi Scientific Centre. It lists whom the community counts as ‘unclean’ dead: those who died unbaptised; suicides (hanged, shot, drowned); women who died in childbirth; stillborn children; sorcerers; missing persons whose bodies were not buried, soldiers excepted; those killed in peacetime; those struck by lightning; those drowned in unclear circumstances; and those who died in the bathhouse (p. 202). The ‘clean’ are those who died old, baptised infants and youths, those who died early but had made confession, and those killed in war, counted as martyrs. The words she records for the unnatural dead are samoubivets, visel’nik, zadavlennik, utoplennik and utopshoi; zalozhnyi does not occur in the text read (p. 201).

The community’s teacher examines each suicide. Church rule forbids commemoration, but he asks whether the man was in health or ‘without mind’; in the second case the dead could be commemorated at common services and a funeral service held some years later, always with the stress that the death was ‘unclean’. Otherwise only the universal services of the parental weeks name them as ‘and those with them’, without their names, though alms may be given (pp. 202–203). The body was not to be touched with bare hands: it was dragged on hooks to a burial place outside the village, usually a low spot, and put in a pit into which went everything used, the rope, work mittens, the hook. People avoided that place. Suicides began to be buried in the cemeteries in the 1970s on the order of the village administration, which angered the Old Believers; many prayer-leaders held the cemeteries defiled, and some saw the ‘last times’ begin. Until recently such dead ‘dropped out’ of the memory of the living, and it was forbidden to speak their names; now families go to the cemetery and cense the graves but do not hold individual commemorations (p. 203).

For an unbaptised child, if the parents’ neglect was the cause, the death counted as the parents’ sin to be confessed. Some held that such souls cannot find rest, others that they are spiritually blind but not sinful and wait in a ‘southern country’ that is not hell. Relatives watched the grave mound for forty days: if the earth stayed still, or rose, it was a sign of the child’s ‘pleasing to God’ state and a six-week requiem could be sung for him as ‘nameless’; if the mound sank, the child had been harmful to people while in the womb and was not commemorated (pp. 203–204). The Old Believers here are book-readers, and an informant answered from a patristic text on those who die suddenly and in water; the teacher’s answer on a man who falls through river ice was that every untimely death must be judged separately. This is one district, one researcher, informants whom she does not name, and a community that reads and weighs written church rules, so its list is an insider’s judgment, not evidence for any wider region (pp. 201–204).

Unbaptised and early dead children: what a Slavic survey allows for Russia

Irina Sedakova’s 2020 article in Folklore 80 surveys the death and funeral of a child across the Slavic world from archive and field data of the late nineteenth to mid-twentieth century. She says that her own records come mainly from Bulgarians in Bulgaria and in diaspora (p. 48). Almost all of what follows is therefore Slavic or Bulgarian, and only a few lines are attributed to Russian places.

Her general statement is that a child who died without baptism and a name was thought to go to hell ‘as if not born’, and that the belief that such children become evil spirits is widespread. She lists the burial places assigned to them (beneath the house, under a window, in the yard, under a tree) without separating the regions in the passage read (p. 56). For baptised children the Russian items are these: in the Russian North a thread as long as the father’s or mother’s height was put in the coffin so that the dead child would not outgrow the parent and ‘walk’ (citing Listova 2001, p. 623); in the Vologda region an egg was put under the pillow of a small child (citing Vlasova 1999, p. 392) (p. 58). Russians, citing Naumenko 1998, p. 116, hold that a child of two or three becomes an angel and one of seven goes to heaven; the fate of an eight-year-old is judged by sins (pp. 61–62).

These are second-hand citations to ethnographic compilations, and the article does not say which village or decade lies behind each. They are enough to show that the fear of the unbaptised dead was Russian as well as southern Slavic. They are not enough for a Russian map of child burial; for that the plate would need the primary regional records.

Two kinds of dead, and the acts that kept the unclean ones out

Svetlana Tolstaya divides the Slavic dead into two groups: the pure, who died a normal death and come back at fixed times without harming anyone, and the unclean (Russian zalozhnye), who died early or by violence, such as suicide, hanging or drowning, or who dealt with evil spirits in life. She adds that the dead of either kind might return because they were neglected, or because they worried about small children, and that the unclean visits were the ones guarded against (pp. 10–11). Her detailed protective measures come mostly from Polesie, which is not Russia: needle sewn right to left on the shroud, coffin carried out feet first through a side door or the window, a stone or branches on the grave, poppy seed and sand scattered for the dead to count until cockcrow, and the aspen stake (pp. 9–11).

For the Russian North she gives one item: after the coffin was carried out, the floor was swept and the house washed, always towards the exit (p. 10). In the same passage she says that in different Slavic traditions the benches and tables were turned over and the water was poured out, and that East Slavs left inverted pots or bowls on graves; those lines are general and name no Russian place (p. 10). Her closing section adds a larger claim, that most characters of ‘lower mythology’ are the souls of such dead, a view she traces to Zelenin’s work on the rusalka (pp. 11–12).

That last claim is disputed in the literature this atlas uses. Dynin, read for plate U, argues that the rusalka image is recent and composite and that Zelenin’s origin from the ‘pledged’ dead explains only part of it. The sentence on the plate therefore stays at what Tolstaya says about the dead, and not at what the figures of folk demonology ‘really’ are.

What Maksimov wrote about suicides and the drowned

Sergei Maksimov’s Nechistaia, nevedomaia i krestnaia sila, published in 1903 by Prince Tenishev’s Ethnographic Bureau after Maksimov’s death, is a general book on spirits by a writer and collector; it gives few sources for individual items and the editors say they completed the manuscript. It is used here for statements that are short and concrete, not as a survey of practice.

On suicides he writes that pious and strict people judged one who laid hands on himself to be ‘the devil’s ram’ (chertu baran), and that the drowned and the hanged were buried, where possible, on the very spot where they had committed the sin, in a bare mound, without a cross and outside the cemetery fence, ‘so that they fall more surely into the power of the unclean’ (pp. 16–17, as marked in the online copy of the 1994 reprint). On water, he says that bruises and scratches on a drowned body showed that the person had been in the vodyanoi’s paws, and that to find a body one set a wooden bowl with three wax candles afloat, and it stopped over the place (pp. 77–78, 83–84). On the wandering of such souls he adds that the girls who died by suicide cannot turn back from the state of a rusalka, and that children smothered by their mothers in sleep passed to the ‘secret people’ and were counted lost unless the mother saved them by forty days of prayer and strict fasting (pp. 20–21, 89).

The three items agree with what the plate gets from Zelenin, Dronova and Tolstaya: a burial away from the churchyard for the self-killed and the belief that the water kept its dead. They add no place and no informant, so each is evidence for what an educated collector of the 1890s held to be the common belief, and not for a named village.

Barsov, writing about the Olonets North in 1872, records that miscarried and unbaptised children were buried outside the cemetery fence in a swamp, and sometimes under the floor of the house; that a suicide in Voezersky parish, Kargopol district, was buried five versts from the church, where passers-by usually laid stones or sticks, burning the sticks when many had gathered (a footnote credits this report to L. Malinovsky); and that those who had hanged themselves in Andoma were buried on a hill among fir trees, face down (pp. 312–313). These are two or three places, not a map, and the Church did not prescribe the acts. They match in kind what the plate has from other regions: a site apart, stones or sticks left by passers-by, a turned body.

Walking dead and literary vampires

Ovseichik cites nineteenth- and early twentieth-century accounts (Dobrovolsky, Bogdanovich) that dead sorcerers walked, killed people and drank their blood, and adds, after Vinogradova and Levkievskaya, that such stories are rare in the East Slavic area and more typical of South Slavic vampire lore. The borderland material is mostly about where and how the impure dead were buried, not about what they did once they rose.

The upyr’ of nineteenth-century Russian fiction is another matter. One thesis abstract places the literary vampire as a device used in Russian writing from the eighteenth century on, built on older belief. The atlas keeps the two apart: the field records read for this plate do not describe a figure like the literary vampire, and nothing here is evidence for one.

Zelenin and his critics

Zelenin’s 1916 compilation gathered reports from many provinces under one heading and read them together as a single belief: that the earth does not accept the dead of ‘unnatural’ death, who therefore stay among the living. He tied the category to the rusalki. The sources read for this plate raise three objections.

Ovseichik will not extend the word ‘zalozhnye’ beyond its Vyatka use, because it is absent from Belarusian and other traditions and because burial methods varied. Avdeev, from epitaphs and letters of church hierarchs, argues that the related superstitions are of Christian times and not pagan survivals; only his abstract was read. Baiburin’s critique of reading rites as survivals of an earlier religion is a general objection to the method, not a verdict on Zelenin’s evidence.

What remains usable is the dated record. The local reports in the compilation, such as the ravine burials on plate N and the Semik citations on plate O, are evidence of what someone wrote down, from whom and where. The unifying belief is Zelenin’s construction. Where the record is read here, it is cited by place.

A dispute about where the rule came from

Ethnographers of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries took such burial customs for pagan survivals. A. Avdeev argues the opposite for Muscovite Russia: from sixteenth to early eighteenth-century epitaphs and letters of church hierarchs he finds no obstacle to cemetery burial or regular commemoration of those who died violent deaths, and he holds that the related superstitions arose in Christian times.

This plate has the abstract only. It cannot say how strong Avdeev’s evidence is or how far his conclusion reaches beyond the Muscovite sources. It is placed here so that the pagan-survival reading is not left standing alone.

A rule remembered, 2018–2019

A. Ryko’s fieldwork in the Khislavichi district of the Smolensk region found only remnants of such beliefs. Informants born in the 1930s remember that those who hanged or drowned themselves were not taken to the common cemetery; younger people say they know nothing of it and that the drowned lay in the cemetery. Unbaptised infants were not counted as ‘unclean’ there. No one now says that certain dead can return and harm the living.

Set beside Ovseichik, the two studies cover neighbouring districts and different decades of the same century, and they do not agree on unbaptised children. The author says the findings rest on memory and that it is not clear whether the trend is local.

THE CALENDAR OF THE DEAD: Church Saturdays, a Thursday the Church lacks and a cemetery day today

A table of who named which day of commemoration, in registers kept apart: the Church, the movable Easter calendar, the Komi-Permyak region and today’s cemetery. The parish is an open gap, and the question of origin is left open.

The Church’s own list

In a church instructional text, Hieromonk Job (Gumerov) names the Saturdays on which the Church commemorates the dead: Meat Fare Saturday, the second, third and fourth Saturdays of Great Lent, Trinity Saturday and St. Demetrius Saturday. He explains the choice of Saturday by its place as the day of rest, and says the Lenten Saturdays stand in for the daily memorial services that are suspended during the fast.

He gives St. Demetrius Saturday a precise origin: it was set up after the battle of Kulikovo in 1380, with the blessing of Sergius of Radonezh, first for the soldiers who fell and later for all the departed. This is the Church’s account of itself, written by a churchman; it is evidence for what the Church holds, not for what a village did.

In Russian village tradition, the universal Saturdays formed an organic seasonal cycle of communion with the ancestral collective (roditeli) (Kremleva 1999, pp. 530–532). On Meat Fare Saturday (spring threshold) and Dmitrievskaya Saturday (autumn threshold), families observed dedicated meals: bliny, hot beer, and pork or mutton pies were served, and the first portion was set aside on the windowsill with the invitation 'Parents, come eat and drink with us'. At the cemetery on Trinity Saturday, graves were ritually swept with fresh birch brooms, and dyed eggs and pies were broken over the turf to share feast-food directly with the dead.

A day counted from Easter

Easter moves from year to year, and the days of commemoration counted from it move with it. A source that says ‘Radonitsa’ or ‘Semik’ is giving a weekday and a week after Easter, not a date of the month. The Church now places Radonitsa on the Tuesday of Thomas week, the ninth day after Easter; that was 14 May in 2024. A reference work of 1913 describes it as falling mostly on the Monday of that week and in some places on the Tuesday, so the day itself varied by place.

Semik is the Thursday of the seventh week after Easter. Counting Easter Sunday as day 0, that is day 46, and Trinity Saturday (day 48) falls two days later; the counting is this plate’s arithmetic, not a statement in the sources. Semik has no place in the Church’s calendar, which is why Maltsev’s Komi-Permyak material and the encyclopedia treat it as a folk day.

Julian and Gregorian reckoning add a second layer: a family’s date for the same day can differ from the date in a modern table. This plate gives no dates for any year.

A day the church calendar does not have

G. I. Maltsev records the commemoration days of the Komi-Permyaks: Radonitsa, Semik, Trinity Saturday, and the Saturdays before Pokrov and St. Demetrius. He writes that Semik, the Thursday of the seventh week after Easter, is absent from the Orthodox calendar, that priests took no part in it in the late nineteenth century and did from the early twentieth. After Zelenin he reports that those dead by unnatural death were commemorated at Semik, and quotes a Kungur saying that they are deaf and blind except on that day.

He also describes a slow move: the Permyaks began to hold this commemoration on the Saturdays of general remembrance, reasoning that prayers would be heard better then. His material is four recordings by one researcher, from a people who are not Russian. The plate uses it only as a regional comparison for a pattern that Russian sources describe.

Read more fully, Maltsev’s article gives Semik among the Permyaks the name dzollun or sharlun and describes it as the day on which those who died unnatural deaths were commemorated, traditionally at the cemetery; for suicides, food was also set at home on a water barrel or on the threshold. Offerings named elsewhere in the article include pancakes and buns taken to graves in a report from Uzhum, afterwards given to the clergy, and pies, eggs and pancakes sent to the other world through a beggar or a river among Permyaks. Priests joined in the early twentieth century (a litia and censing of graves is cited from 1904), and today a memorial service for all the departed is held on Semik in the Svyato-Stefanovsky deanery of the Kudymkar diocese. The article rests on published sources, on material from the Kungur and Komi Zyuzda districts, and on the author’s own fieldwork in the Yusva, Gayny and Kosa districts between 1995 and 2016.

A cemetery day, 2016–2017

E. G. Chesnokova’s fieldwork in the Kostroma and Vladimir regions found the main cemetery commemoration falling in either the Easter or the Trinity period, and she says this corresponds broadly to certain regional zones, which the abstract does not name. Within the period one day carries the main rites, and she divides it into a preparation, a meeting of relatives at the cemetery, a walk among the graves, commemoration at the grave, and a continuation at home.

She finds these days both family occasions and social ones, with their importance held or growing, and says collective commemoration repairs ties weakened by distance and broken family traditions. The abstract does not name the day, and the counts of respondents are not given.

What the three sources do not settle

The sources do not say where the folk days came from. A common account makes Radonitsa and Semik pagan spring feasts of the dead that the Church absorbed, but none of the three read here supports or refutes that, and the one popular essay on the subject that came up in searching was not used. What they show is narrower: two calendars that overlap, one the Church’s and one regional, and a regional practice that was moved toward the Church’s Saturdays.

The atlas therefore marks the origin question as contested and leaves it open. The calendar here is a table of who named which day, not a history of how the days arose.

Burying the rusalka at Bolshaya Vereika

In the village of Bolshaya Vereika, Zemlyansk district, Voronezh oblast, women and girls ‘led’ or ‘buried’ a rusalka each year. N. P. Grinkova collected the rite twice, in 1925 and in July 1936, mainly from the storyteller A. K. Baryshnikova, who directed it in performance; she also acquired the figure and its props for the State Museum of Ethnography (collection no. 5866, 1936). The figure was a sheaf of reed (kuga), less often of straw, about a metre high, with cross-stalks for arms, a white cloth for a face, and a woman’s embroidered shirt, skirt and headscarf. It lay on a stretcher under a white cloth and three embroidered towels, so that only the head showed, like a dead woman laid out.

The procession set out from the house where the figure was dressed, in holiday clothes, with reed stems for candles. One woman took the part of the priest: hair loose, a mat over her shoulders for a chasuble, and a worn bast shoe swung as a censer. She led a dance-tune song through the village and the others took up the refrain. In the rye field the figure was stripped, the towels and ribbons removed, and figure and stretcher poles thrown into the ditch at the field’s edge. The day was the zagovenye after Trinity, the last Sunday before the Apostles’ Fast.

Baryshnikova gave two reasons: thrown into the rye, the rusalka makes the bread grow better; and the rite protects from rusalkas, who would frighten people all summer if it were not done. Women of the older generation took it seriously, and before 1917 the estate steward let women off work for it. The women said it was last done in 1935; in spring 1936 ‘somehow they did not gather’, and in July 1936 Grinkova saw only a staged reconstruction.

The comparisons and the frame are Grinkova’s own: she places the rite among ‘burials of Kostroma’ and the cuckoo, cites Zelenin’s records of rusalka processions in Serdobsk district (Saratov province) and in Ryazan province, and reads all of them as survivals of a dying-and-resurrecting deity cult after Frazer. That interpretation is not adopted here. The record itself is a single village, recorded by one ethnographer from one main informant (pp. 178–181).

The rusalka: a southern name that reached the North late

Vladimir Dynin’s 1994 article in Etnograficheskoe obozrenie sets out how the East Slavs described the rusalka, using published provincial reports of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and recent expedition notes. In the south of Russia, in Ukraine and in part of Belarus she is mostly a young, beautiful girl with loose, long hair, and she is usually the soul of a drowned woman or of a child who died unbaptised; these are the places linked to the Trinity–Semik season and the rites of ‘seeing off’ the rusalka. In the Russian North, he says, the same word was not known until recently: correspondents of Tenishev from northern provinces said so, and Zelenin showed that it arrived there in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries with town culture. Northern informants described a shaggy woman with long hanging breasts who combs her hair on a rock or a snag and who is called by other names: vodyanikha, chertovka, leshachikha. One recorded remark from the Kholmogory district runs that ‘now they call them rusalki, and before it was all chert’ (pp. 112–113).

His conclusions are that the figure is missing from northern demonology, that it was still unsettled in the south, and that the earliest reports of the rusalka as such date from the eighteenth century. He therefore holds that the image is recent and was built from several beings: poludnitsy, wood and water spirits, and the dead honoured in ‘rusalia’ week. He derives the name from rusalia, a term he links to the Roman Rosalia memorial rite, and he adds that the cult of the ‘pledged’ dead, as Zelenin noted, spread widely in the eighteenth to twentieth centuries (pp. 113–114, 117–118).

This is one side of an open argument. Dynin himself lists others who took the rusalka as an ancient nature spirit, as the dead ancestors, or as a female deity of moisture, and he says that none of these views has been generally accepted (p. 111). The plate therefore uses the article for what it records about regions and names, and treats the late-origin thesis as contested. Pages are taken from the running heads of the supplied PDF.

Two aims, and the acts that serve them

An encyclopedia article on East Slavic funeral rites says the rite works in two directions: some acts help the dead reach the other world, others protect the living from the dead. Its protective and cleansing acts include washing the house after the coffin is carried out, turning objects over, covering mirrors and washing hands on returning from the cemetery. Its prohibitions include close relatives making the coffin and bringing food back from the cemetery.

The explanation the article gives is general: the dead can harm the living, and the boundary between worlds stays open from the death until the burial and the fortieth day. It does not give a reason for each separate act, and it does not date its examples. The page does not name an author. The page has no bibliography, but it names its authorities in the text: Baiburin (1993), Razumova (2012), Tolstaya (2009, 2019), Spassky (1877) and an 1890 collection of materials on daily life. Washing as removal of the marks of the living is attributed to Baiburin; the epistolary character of laments in the Russian North to Tolstaya.

Barsov (1872, p. 312) lists acts done so as not to fear the dead: touching the dead man’s heels, kissing him hard at the farewell, looking at the legs of the horse that carries him from under the shafts on the way to the cemetery, touching the house stove with the fingers on returning, and, if he still disturbs the living with apparitions, driving him off with a bridle on the Svir. He gives them as known customs from different places, not as one rite, and attaches no reason to any act beyond the stated aim. He adds that when someone dies without repentance, his relatives bow at the feet of everyone who comes to church, and sometimes of anyone they meet within two or three weeks, asking forgiveness of the dead person’s sins, in the belief that the sins pass to the living who take part in the singing or are bowed to; and that some Kargopol parishes have no custom of commemoration at all, with a saying that one does not stir a dead bone.

In the house

J. Rouhier-Willoughby, in a review of urban funerals, lists what the older village rite is reported to have included: candles lit, all the mirrors in the house covered, all water thrown out. She cites the ethnographers for covering the mirrors so that the soul would not be trapped, for discarding the water because it could trap the soul, and for mirrors being seen as symbolically like water because they reflect. The women who washed the body turned tables and benches over before washing them, she writes, so that death would enter the soil; a priest might also say a prayer of purification once the body had been carried out.

She draws these from earlier ethnographers, so the village custom reaches the atlas at second hand. For the urban twentieth century she reports that the covering of shiny surfaces continued.

Washing

Washing the body appears in both sources as an obligatory part of the rite. The encyclopedia cites a scholar who argues that the strictness of the rule points to a purpose beyond cleaning: removing what marked the person as living. In the Russian North, it says, the washing was done by specially invited people, usually women past childbearing age.

In the Khislavichi district of Smolensk, A. Ryko’s informants said neighbours were invited to wash, women washing women and men washing men, and she found no stated ban on relatives taking part. The encyclopedia’s prohibition concerns making the coffin, not washing; Ryko’s article names no ban on relatives for washing, and says that excluding close relatives would fit the general boundary function of washing, as a wider pattern and not as a rule recorded locally. The two sources describe different places and cannot be added together.

A 1927 survey: what was done in the house

Zelenin’s survey of East Slavic ethnography (1927), § 132, lists the acts around a death. At the moment of death a vessel of water was set beside the dying person; many said they had seen the water stir, as if from the bathing of the soul that had just left. It stood on the table or windowsill for forty days, and people held that during that time the soul had not yet gone to the other world, and some that it drank the water. At the same moment the stove flues or a small window were opened and a cloth or towel hung out of the window. People explained the cloth in different ways: the soul dries itself on it, wipes its tears with it, or rests on it. Zelenin suggests it first eased the soul’s way out of the house and back. A long death agony was put down to dealings with unclean power, and then the roof ridge was broken or a ceiling board lifted so that the soul could leave. Copper coins were laid on the eyes so that the dead should not look at a member of the family, who would then die. The bed on which someone had died was thrown out or put in the hen house for the cock to cleanse.

When the coffin was brought in, everyone left the house, pregnant women above all; they also left when it was carried out and were not to wash the body, or their child would die. In Olonets province, he writes, citing Barsov, the coffin went out through the window so that the dead could not find the way back; it was carried feet first and struck three times on the threshold so that the dead would take leave of the old home. Water standing in the house at the moment of death was poured out, because the dead might have drunk it. A woman followed the procession with a bath broom, sweeping the floor to the threshold and sprinkling water, and the floor was then washed. Zelenin reads this as magic, not hygiene, and gives the informants’ wording: the water is poured ‘to wash away the dead man’s traces’. While the body lay in the house the floor was not swept, ‘so as not to sweep out the living’; afterwards the rubbish was swept toward the house, so that its people would stay. Rye and barley were thrown behind the procession and on the coffin ‘so that no one in the house dies again’. An axe or knife was struck on the bench where the dead had lain, ‘to cut off death’. Bread was not baked while the body was in the house, and no sowing or planting was allowed in the village until the burial, or the crops would not come up.

Zelenin merges Russian, Ukrainian and Belarusian material, and I have kept to the items he gives for Russians or the Russian North or leaves general. His details come from the archive of the Russian Geographical Society and from regional collectors, cited by volume and number; the archive files were not seen. He gives no dates. The passages read do not mention covering mirrors, so the mirror material on this plate rests on the three other sources.

Done without an explanation

Ryko writes that people in the borderland often perform funeral actions without being able to say what they are for, and that as the motives fade the sense of obligation fades with them. This is her observation of one district, based on informants’ memories, and she says it is unclear whether the trend is local.

It matters here because the reasons attached to protective acts in the other sources are the reasons collected from or ascribed to people at particular times. They are not a fixed meaning that every act always carried.

Why the mirror?

Several reasons are on record for covering mirrors. A hieromonk, writing against the custom, reports that people said the soul should not be frightened by its reflection, or the dead should not frighten the family, or the soul might slip into the world behind the mirror, which he says the devil rules. Rouhier-Willoughby, reporting earlier ethnographers, gives the reason that the soul should not be trapped, and ties mirrors to water, which was also thrown out because it could trap the soul.

These do not agree with each other and none is shown here to be the original. The church writer is hostile and gives the folk reasons in order to dismiss them. The atlas lists the reasons and leaves the meaning of the act unsettled.

Ethnolinguistic field evidence gathered across Russian provinces (Tolstaya 1999, pp. 321–324) documents the underlying structural logic of covering mirrors. In peasant worldview, the mirror is an active, permeable boundary between this world and the otherworld. The primary fear recorded among informants is the 'doubling' of death (udvoenie smerti): if the corpse or any living household member is reflected, death will inevitably strike a second person in the home. Secondarily, the unquiet soul, wandering about the house during the initial forty days, is thought vulnerable to entrapment inside the glass matrix, unable to cross over. Looking into an uncovered mirror while a corpse remains in the house was subject to an absolute taboo, threatening sickness or immediate death.

The Church’s position

Hieromonk Dometian calls the covering of mirrors a folk custom with nothing in common with the Orthodox burial rite, and he says the same of overturning tables and chairs, which he calls pagan survivals. He also reports a belief that one must not look out of a window at a funeral procession, and he tells believers to take the blessing of a priest as their only guide.

This is a polemical text of unstated date. It shows what the Church objects to, and by implication what was practised, but it is not a count of practice.

COLLECTIVE DEATH: Plague, war, and the state’s arithmetic of the dead

Mass death produces two things at once: a hole in the record and an official number. The atlas is interested in the gap between them.

1771: the pit

The Moscow plague of 1771 killed many thousands of people; the municipal note used here gives no total. The dead were buried outside the city, and the new cemetery at Vagankovo received, first of all, unnamed people. The historical account of the cemetery begins with a number it cannot disaggregate.

Mass death produces this kind of record: a pit, a total, a name for the place, no names for the people. The atlas does not illustrate the pit. It prints the fact of it.

Archival demographic studies of the 1770–1772 Moscow plague estimate total mortality between 50,000 and 100,000 victims, precipitating a permanent transformation of Russian burial geography. The Senate decrees of 24 March and 1 November 1771 marked the first absolute civil prohibition against churchyard interments in Russian cities. To prevent pestilence, the municipal administration mandated the creation of dedicated burial grounds beyond the Kamer-Kollezhsky customs rampart—founding Vagankovo, Danilovskoe, Miusskoe, and Rogozhskoe as mass sanitary burial tracts where thousands of plague dead were interred in common lime-dusted trenches.

The unknown soldier

The Tomb of the Unknown Soldier at the Kremlin wall was opened on 8 May 1967. The monument was designed by the architects Dmitrii Burdin, Vladimir Klimov and Iurii Rabaev, with the sculptor Nikolai Tomskii; the remains had been brought from a mass grave outside Moscow. The eternal flame was lit at the opening and has been maintained since.

The unknown soldier is a very specific solution to a problem of scale: it converts an unmanageable number into a single body that can be visited. What it gains in ritual focus it loses in information. The state’s memory is built on that trade.

Unburied

Alexander Etkind’s Warped Mourning argues that Soviet culture was shaped by deaths that were not buried properly — the executed, the disappeared, the dead in unmarked ground — and that literature and memory-work in Russia have repeatedly returned to them as the ‘undead’. The argument is a cultural history, not a survey of graves, and the atlas presents it as an interpretation.

Catherine Merridale’s Night of Stone makes a related point from the side of the living: the twentieth century in Russia produced so much death that mourning itself became a skill that had to be managed, suppressed, or performed in private. Both books are used here for their arguments, not as inventories of fact.

‘Buried well’: a village formula and the funeral photograph

Oleg Nikolaev, a philologist at the Memorial society, wrote in 2012 about funeral photographs in Russian family albums. From his fieldwork he reports that in some archives they make up to 20% of all pictures, and that they peak in the 1950s–1970s: at the open coffin, on the lorry to the cemetery, at the grave, at the memorial table. He thinks the practice belongs to villages, small provincial towns and city people of village origin, and that it had largely left use by the 1980s (pp. 358–359, 361, 369–370). The three photographs reproduced in the article come from family archives: one from Bashkortostan (a funeral of the 1930s) and two from a village in the Chelyabinsk region (pp. 359–361).

His central observation is a phrase. In his account, a funeral is judged by the formula ‘buried well’ (pokhoronili khorosho), and stories about funerals are usually built on it and most often begin or end with it. It covers correct ritual from washing to the memorial meal, a good place near relatives, a tended grave, telegrams sent to the family and many people coming to take leave, and also that the grief was told and heard (p. 365). He found the same phrase in a soldier’s letter of 25 September 1943, where a comrade tells a widow that her husband was buried well and that the grave was edged with turf and a marker set up; the letter is held in the digital archive of the ‘Museum of Folk Writing’ programme (p. 368). He also prints in full a written account of 1984 by his grandfather, Fyodor Vinogradov, of the death and burial of his wife, and reads it as the formula unfolded point by point (pp. 365–367).

From this he argues that the photograph served the same formula in visual form. His reasoning: after the war, deportation and the camps many families knew of relatives who had gone missing or lain in mass or nameless graves, and a photograph proved that this family could bury its own dead, and bury them correctly. Prints were sent to relatives in other towns, which let a scattered kin group see who stood by the coffin (pp. 368–369). This is his hypothesis, put forward as a supposition, and it rests on his own expedition collections, which the article does not list or count. The plate uses it as an interpretation beside Etkind and Merridale, and does not treat it as a measured fact.

Trinity Saturday at a mass grave: Yurla district, Perm region, 2013–2015

A team from Perm State University (Svetlana Koroleva, Elena Chetina, Oksana Kolegova) gathered interviews and photographs in the Yurla district of the Perm region, a Russian-speaking area among Komi-Permyaks, in 2013–2015. Their article describes how places linked to the Civil War and to the settling of special deportees (1919 to the early 1950s) came into local commemoration. Their finding is that the places fall on a spectrum between official and popular forms, and that where the popular form prevails the mass grave is placed in the calendar commemoration of one’s own dead, which turns from a family rite into a shared one.

Their clearest case is Ust-Pyshya, a logging settlement founded in the late 1940s or early 1950s that ceased to exist in 2014. Until then its residents held a multi-stage commemoration in Trinity week: first the graves of their relatives at the cemetery near the village of Bulychi, then the obelisk at the mass grave of fourteen people shot there by Whites in 1919, whose names are mostly unknown, and then a cemetery of Polish special settlers, always in nearly the same order. All informants the authors spoke to said they went to the grave on Trinity Saturday and not on revolutionary holidays. They censed their own relatives’ graves, then those of people with no one to commemorate, then the obelisk, brought food, and called the dead simply the ‘killed’. One woman explained the visits by saying that nobody else commemorated them. The authors also report that other Yurla places linked to bloodshed or abandoned cemeteries were understood as ‘unclean’ and dangerous.

The evidence is interview recollection in one district, with the authors’ own count of informants not stated in the part read, and the narrators of the mythological stories are withheld by the authors’ own note. It shows a documented case of an ordinary calendar rite extended to the war dead of a settlement that no longer exists. It cannot tell how common the practice is in Russia; the plate keeps it beside Nikolaev’s village formula as a second regional observation.

Butovo: a count with four numbers

The Butovo firing range south of Moscow is the plate’s case of mass death that was administered, not suffered by accident. A memorial site run by the church says it is the largest place of mass shootings and burials of victims of Stalinist repression in the Moscow region, that 20,760 names are known, that the shootings fell between August 1937 and October 1938, and that the range worked from 1934 to 1953. The Great Russian Encyclopedia, read only in extract, gives about 20.8 thousand executed and buried in those months, of them 19.9 thousand men, over 80 per cent non-party. Other pages read give 20,761, 20,762 and 20,765. The atlas does not choose among them. The spread is small. The procession of portraits

Collective death is also remembered from below, and the best-documented recent case began in Siberia. According to Meduza, on 9 May 2012 six thousand people in Tomsk walked with portraits of their relatives who fought in the war; the form was taken up in about 140 places in 2013 and about 450 in 2014. The same article is mainly about a dispute over who owns the name and the procession, which this plate does not follow. THE SOVIET AFTERLIFE: Cremation, the civil funeral, and the industry that followed

Civil funerals without a priest, cremation in a former church, and a funeral market, seen mostly through Moscow sources.

A crematorium in a church

Moscow’s first crematorium was fitted into a church building at the Donskoy Monastery, the church of St Seraphim of Sarov and St Anna of Kashin. A Moscow city-guide page calls it an unfinished church never consecrated before the revolution; an Orthodox website calls it a rebuilt church. According to the archive site Tehne, an exhibition of crematorium models at the Moscow Communal Economy museum in January 1925 was followed by a design competition; quoting a 1926 magazine, it names three entries (Dyakonov, Melnikov, Osipov), gives first prize to Osipov, and says the jury asked for a building that did not look like a church inside or out. A hobbyist page also names Dmitry Osipov as the architect of the conversion. The furnace, the Orthodox site writes, came from the German firm Topf and Sons.

The dates differ by a day or so. Sokolova gives 12 January 1927 as the opening; the hobbyist page gives a trial burning of a worker’s body on 11 January, and Tehne dates a first trial cremation to 29 December 1926. The official opening is 6 October 1927 in the Memorial database and on Tehne (which adds that the first scheduled cremation was held that day), 7 October on the hobbyist page.

The building was later used for the terror. Memorial’s Topos database records that bodies of people shot in Moscow were cremated at Donskoy, documented from 1934, with earlier cases possible, and that the ashes were buried in the New Donskoy cemetery.

The civil funeral

A Soviet funeral had no priest. In the 1920s the ‘red funeral’ was a procession to the cemetery led by musicians, with Komsomol members carrying a red coffin, then flags and revolutionary songs, and at the grave a civil memorial service with an honour guard and speeches. Sokolova concludes that such funerals were rare in the 1920s, the political act of a narrow circle of young activists.

Her figures: in Moscow, 39.9% of funerals in 1925 and 40.4% in 1926 were held without a church service, so about six in ten still had one; in Tambov province, nine civil funerals in the first quarter of 1927 for the whole province. She writes that the 1960s brought a state campaign for socialist non-religious rites, with local commissions, and that funeral rites appear in only some of its guideline collections. The article was read in summary only.

The institutional secularization of death proceeded through distinct legal stages. The Council of People's Commissars decree of 7 December 1918 abolished church jurisdiction over cemeteries, placing burial grounds under municipal Soviets and initiating civil funeral ceremonies with orchestral accompaniment and revolutionary speeches. This bureaucratic monopoly reached its architectural culmination in 1927 with the Donskoy Crematorium. Following the collapse of the USSR, Federal Law No. 8-FZ of 12 January 1996 ('On Burial and Funeral Business') dismantled state monopoly: it established a state-guaranteed minimum service package (garantirovannyi perechen') for every citizen, while opening cemetery maintenance, transport, and monument fabrication to private commercial funeral enterprises.

The funeral market, Moscow 1996–2012

The federal law of 12 January 1996 ‘On Burial and Funeral Business’ guarantees relatives a list of burial services free of charge, provided by a specialised service (Article 9). In June 2012 (21 June), The Moscow Times reports, the head of the city’s trade and services department said commercial functions, such as digging graves and transporting coffins, would pass from the municipal company Ritual to private firms. The same report puts the Moscow burial-services market at more than 12 billion rubles, cremation at 44% of Moscow burials, and the number of cemeteries at 71, only two of them open to new burials.

These are Moscow figures from one newspaper report and say nothing about the rest of Russia. The sources read do not cover the years 1991–95 or after 2012, and they do not show how church funerals returned.

THE UNFINISHED ARCHIVE: What is missing, and how to draw it

A plate of absences: graves, registers and sources the atlas could not find or read.

The record has holes

Moscow’s cemeteries were closed in the twentieth century for reasons that a 2013 newspaper list gives as sanitary norms, factory and plant building, parks, housing, a metro line and tram tracks. In some cases relatives were offered reburial; in others most of the dead were not moved. The list says nothing about burial registers.

This plate collects the forms of absence the atlas has met: a grave whose monument has disappeared, a cemetery photographed but not surveyed, a source that could not be read, a practice described only in a footnote.

The destruction of Russian vital records is systematically traced by archival historians (Antonov and Antonova 2006, pp. 112–148). From the early eighteenth century until 1918, parish clergy maintained metric books in two copies: a local parish register and an annual fair copy dispatched to the diocesan consistory. The Soviet decree of 18 December 1917 transferred registration to newly founded civil ZAGS bureaus, stripping the Church of legal authority. During the antireligious campaigns, church closures, and paper shortages of the 1920s and 1930s, tens of thousands of local parish copies were discarded, burned, or pulped; in western provinces, wartime devastation in 1941–1944 destroyed entire municipal and ZAGS archives. Surviving consistory copies, now preserved in regional state archives, suffer from major chronological chasms, leaving entire rural districts devoid of documented burial registers before the mid-nineteenth century.

Novosibirsk: dated decisions, as reported

In a 2023 interview, E. Krasilnikova of Novosibirsk State Technical University dates a run of local decisions. A 1920 decision concerned cutting the grove at the cemetery. In 1929 the Voskresenskaya church was ordered closed, and in April 1930 a crowd of about a thousand protested and was dispersed by the militia. The old cemetery became a garden and later part of a park; the church went to the park administration, held a planetarium and burned in the 1970s.

She places the start of such demolitions in Siberia in the mid-1920s. The interview states the dates as facts but cites no file or archive for them, so they stand here as the interviewee’s report.

Barnaul and Omsk

In her account two large cemeteries in Barnaul were destroyed in the 1930s, graves were opened in search of valuables, which she says a local government decree backed, and bones were left scattered. She calls Barnaul the most radical case in Siberia; that comparison is her judgement.

Why the cemeteries went

The interview names several kinds of motive, which this plate groups into four, and which the interview does not itself list as four: ideological (shedding pre-revolutionary culture and building a socialist city), practical (neglected graves after the Civil War, typhus burials, no relatives left to tend them), symbolic (local notables buried there whom the new authorities saw no need to remember) and emotional (funerals used to mobilise people, revolutionary rites replacing religious ones). Documented property histories and municipal renovation records explain the destruction of urban necropolises as an ideological and utilitarian program. Consecrated ground was cleared to make way for palaces of culture and parks of culture and rest.

PLATE Y · 1941 to present · Novgorod, Leningrad, Rzhev and Stalingrad battlegrounds

THE MILITARY CENOTAPH: Mass graves, the unknown soldier, search expeditions, and unburied bones

Battlefield mass burials, sanitary trenches, the official cult of the Unknown Soldier, and the search expeditions recovering remains from peat bogs eighty years after the frontlines froze.

The wartime mass grave

In March 1941, People’s Commissar of Defence Order No. 138 established standard burial regulations for Red Army casualties: soldiers were to be buried in individual graves or common fraternal trenches (bratskie mogily), with a minimum depth of 1.5 metres (2.0 metres in waterlogged soils) and wooden markers bearing name, rank, and unit.

Under front-line combat conditions during the 1941–1942 retreats, encirclements, and winter fighting, these regulations broke down completely. Battlefield casualties were interred in bomb craters, anti-tank ditches, and hasty sanitary pits with little or no documentation. Bakelite identification capsules (smertnye medaliony), instituted in 1941, were frequently discarded by soldiers who feared them as bad omens, leaving millions of bodies nameless.

The cenotaph and the Unknown Soldier

Because mass graves could not deliver individual remains to families, Soviet commemorative policy pivoted toward the monumental cenotaph: an empty tomb or symbolic marker standing for the unreturned body. In December 1966, to commemorate the 25th anniversary of the Battle of Moscow, the remains of an unidentified soldier were exhumed from a mass grave at kilometre 41 of the Leningrad Highway and transferred to the Alexander Garden at the Kremlin wall.

Dedicated on 8 May 1967, the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier established the formula: 'Your name is unknown, your deed is immortal' (Imia tvoe neizvestno, podvig tvoi bessmerten). The tomb functioned as a secular reliquary, substituting a single collective body for the uncounted millions whose actual resting places were unrecorded forest depressions.

The search movement: Valley of Death

From the late 1960s, informal grassroots expeditions—later known as the search movement (poiskoviki)—began excavating battlefield zones where thousands of Red Army soldiers lay unburied on the surface. The epicentre was the Lyuban pocket at Myasnoi Bor ('The Valley of Death') in Novgorod Oblast, where the 2nd Shock Army was encircled and annihilated in June 1942.

Initiated by amateur searcher Nikolai Orlov and later formalized as the Dolina expedition, volunteers documented tens of thousands of skeletal remains in the moss and mud. The movement operated under the moral maxim attributed to Generalissimo Suvorov: 'A war is not over until the last soldier is buried.'

Arithmetic: official lists versus the ground

Tumarkin and Merridale trace the divergence between the state’s monumental accounting and the actual geography of the dead. The official Ministry of Defence database (OBD Memorial) records millions of names and mapped cemeteries, yet field expeditions continue to unearth thousands of unrecorded remains each season across Novgorod, Leningrad, Tver, and Smolensk regions.

The military cenotaph resolves this material contradiction ideologically: where the physical bones are dissolved in peat or buried in unmarked sand, granite and bronze erect an immutable boundary of memory.

Memory work and contested commemoration

Etkind (2013) distinguishes between 'hard' and 'soft' mourning in post-Soviet Russia: hard mourning erects monuments and fixes names; soft mourning works in literature, scholarship and private ceremony. The official cult of the Unknown Soldier is a form of hard mourning, but it coexists with what Etkind calls 'mourning work' in unofficial registers.

Koroleva et al. (2015) document how localities around Perm built their own sites of commemoration around mass graves identified by search parties—sites that pre-date and sometimes conflict with the official memorial landscape. These local memorials, made without state permission, are a form of soft mourning that the state has not always tolerated.

PLATE Z · 18th to 21st century · Rural churchyards and decommissioned municipal cemeteries

OBLIVION AND RECLAMATION: Closed cemeteries, sinking mounds, erased inscriptions, and the quiet return to soil

The material end of the cemetery: sanitary closures, the collapse of wooden crosses, the chemical weathering of limestone names, and the return of memorial ground to forest and turf.

The sanitary lifecycle of the cemetery

In modern Russian sanitary law (SanPiN 2.1.2882-11), a cemetery is not an eternal monument but an allocated land plot subject to an institutional lifecycle. Once burial capacity is exhausted, the municipality declares the cemetery 'closed' (zakrytoe dlia zakhoronenii).

A statutory 'cemetery period' (kladbishchenskii period)—defined as the time required for complete decomposition of biological tissues, set at 20 years for standard soil conditions—must elapse before the terrain may be considered stable. Even after closure, the law forbids residential or commercial construction on former cemetery grounds, allowing only green parks and memorial groves.

Decay of matter: timber, turf and sinkholes

In the rural North and central regions, the physical survival of a grave depends entirely on periodic kin maintenance. Unpainted spruce and pine crosses rot at the groundline within 25 to 40 years. Permilovskaya notes that traditional roofed crosses (golubtsy) protected the icon plate, yet moisture rising from the subsoil inevitably rots the base.

As the pine coffin collapses under ground pressure (usually between the 7th and 15th year), the earthen mound subsides, creating a hollow indentation. When family lines die out or migrate to cities, grave weeding ceases; nettles, cow parsnip, and birch seedlings colonize the depression, transforming individual burial markers into uniform forest turf.

Erased stone and unreadable names

Stone inscriptions do not guarantee permanent memory. Soft white limestone slabs, common in central Russia and Pskov from the 16th to the 19th century, weather through rain, freeze-thaw cycles, and lichen growth, losing Cyrillic letterforms within a century and a half.

By the third or fourth generation after burial, the emotional link to individual dead fades into the anonymous category of 'ancestors' (roditeli). The individual grave ceases to be visited as a person; it dissolves into the communal cemetery ground.

The cemetery as ecological threshold

Where modern memorialization attempts to freeze time through polished granite and digital servers, traditional rural practice accepted oblivion as a natural material transition. The grave was expected to settle into the soil as the soul departed to the ancestors.

Pushkin's 1836 elegy on the village churchyard articulates this exact reconciliation: the decay of the wooden cross under ancient oaks is not seen as desecration or defeat, but as peaceful reconciliation with mother earth.

SYSTEMATIC APPARATUS III

Chthonic Herbarium & Funerary Dendrology

  • Populus tremula

    European Aspen

    Chthonic tree of execution and impalement; pinning weapon against unquiet corpses (zalozhnye pokoiniki).

    According to apocryphal legends recorded across northern and central Russia, the aspen's leaves tremble perpetually because Judas Iscariot hanged himself upon it, or because it refused to mourn during the Crucifixion. It was strictly taboo to build residential log cabins (izbas) with aspen logs; doing so invited chronic illness, spirit hauntings, and untimely death. Conversely, a sharpened aspen stake (osinovyi kol) driven through the spine or grave mound of an upyr, sorcerer, or suicide was the primary mechanical and ritual means to physically pin the revenant to the earth and break its post-mortem mobility.

  • Picea abies

    Norway Spruce / Spruce Boughs

    Liminal border tree marking the realm of ancestors; path-masking barrier against post-mortem return.

    The spruce forest is the archaic Russian locus of death and ancestral spirits. Idiomatic euphemisms for burial include 'to go under the spruce' (ugodit' pod el') or 'to look under the fir tree' (smotret' pod elochku). During the funeral procession, cut green spruce boughs (lapnik) were strewn thickly behind the cart or sled carrying the coffin from the deceased's gate all the way to the cemetery fence. This served a triple apotropaic function: honoring the deceased with an evergreen carpet, masking the volatile stench of cadaveric decomposition, and pricking the bare soles of the wandering soul so it could not find its way back to the family dwelling.

  • Betula pendula

    Silver Birch & Birch Bark

    Waterproof mortuary lining and archaic northern shroud; arboreal receptacle for maiden souls.

    Before sawn planks and commercial textiles became ubiquitous, northern peasants and Old Believer communities wrapped corpses in large, pliable sheets of stripped birch bark (beresta) or lined the bottom of monoxyle log coffins (domovinas) with birch bark mats. Birch bark provided thermal and aqueous insulation from frozen or waterlogged permafrost. In spring rites of Semik and Trinity week, maiden birch saplings were decorated with ribbons and 'curled' into arches, serving as temporary arboreal vessels for the restless souls of drowned girls and unmarried maidens (rusalki).

  • Juniperus communis

    Common Juniper

    Sacred fumigant and antiseptic purifier of spaces contaminated by cadaveric miasma.

    Immediately following the removal of the coffin from the peasant izba, dried juniper branches were ignited on an iron hearth shovel or in a clay pot. The heavy, aromatic resinous smoke was wafted through every room, under the benches, and across the threshold. Folk medicine regarded juniper smoke as a disinfectant against pestilence and typhus, while ritual belief held that its pungent fumes expelled the lingering 'spirit of the corpse' (mertvechinnyi dukh) and disoriented any malevolent minor spirits (kikimoras, banniks) attracted by death.

  • Papaver rhoeas / somniferum

    Wild Poppy Seeds

    Innumerable counting barrier impeding nocturnal predatory revenants at thresholds and crossroads.

    Uncultivated, wild poppy seeds were scattered inside the coffin beneath the mortuary shroud, along the domestic threshold, and at road intersections where the funeral cortege paused. Under Slavic folklore topology, a walking corpse, revenant, or vampire possessed an obsessive, involuntary compulsion to gather and tally every scattered grain before crossing a perimeter. Because the thousands of microscopic seeds could not be gathered before the first cockcrow, dawn broke and neutralised the entity's supernatural locomotion.

  • Artemisia absinthium

    Absinthe Wormwood

    Aromatic plant talisman against watery revenants and seductive forest spirits during Semik.

    Wormwood is the foremost botanical counter-charm against rusalki and water maidens. During the perilous Rusalnaia Week preceding Trinity Sunday, peasants never entered forests, riverbanks, or marshes without a bunch of pungent wormwood tucked into their belt or pocket. A recurrent riddle-narrative recorded in folklore recounts a rusalka confronting a traveler with the binary question: 'Wormwood or parsley?' (Polyn' ili petrushka?). Answering 'Parsley!' caused the spirit to cry 'You are my darling!' and tickle the traveler to death; answering 'Wormwood!' caused her to shriek 'Perish and fade!' and flee into the reeds.

  • Sorbus aucuparia

    European Rowan / Mountain Ash

    Amuletic threshold guardian whose scarlet berries and wood repel witchcraft and cattle plague.

    Twig crosses made from rowan branches tied with crimson wool yarn were fastened above windows and stable doors after a death in the homestead. During nocturnal village-ploughing rituals (opakhivanie) against epizootic cattle plagues, young maidens brandished rowan sticks to beat the boundary perimeter. Rowan wood was forbidden to be chopped carelessly for ordinary firewood; breaking a branch near a graveyard was believed to cause severe toothache or invite a curse from the dead.

  • Linum usitatissimum

    Common Flax

    Canonical unbleached mortuary textile; strict prohibition of dyed synthetic fabrics or metal fasteners.

    The mortuary shroud (savan) and the death belt (pokromka) had to be crafted exclusively from hand-spun, unbleached pure linen. Flax was cultivated and woven by elderly post-menopausal women who had observed ritual ablutions. The mortuary robe was sewn with basting stitches from right to left, without making a single knot (knots were believed to bind the soul and impede its ascent through aerial toll-houses) and without touching any iron needles or shears once completed. Any trace of dyed calico, wool, or metallic fasteners was strictly banned as an unclean worldly contagion.
