  1. A 01 Death as a Threshold The ethnography of death in Russia is rarely an account of dying. It is an account of what the living do in the room afterwards — and of how quickly that room becomes a border. Documented mainly 19th–20th centuries
    European Russia; unevenly recorded     OPEN PLATE →
  2. B 02 The Body Before it becomes a memory, the dead is an object that must be handled. The handling is the ritual. Documented 19th–20th centuries
    European Russia     OPEN PLATE →
  3. C 03 The Ritual The most confidently repeated ‘Russian funeral custom’ is also the one most often misdescribed: the commemoration on the third, ninth and fortieth day is a liturgical scheme before it is a folk one. Liturgical sources 17th–19th c.; practice recorded 19th–21st c.
    Russia; Orthodox parishes     OPEN PLATE →
  4. D 04 The Table The memorial meal is the place where the dead is most directly treated as a person with preferences, a place and an appetite. Documented 19th–21st centuries
    Russia; one Ukrainian example, marked     OPEN PLATE →
  5. E 05 The Returning Dead Not every dead person was thought to stay dead. The distinction was not good and evil but proper and improper death — and the category that names it has been under dispute for a century. Recorded 19th–early 20th c.; category proposed 1916
    European Russia; regional variation     OPEN PLATE →
  6. F 06 The Grave The Russian grave is a legal object before it is a sacred one. Where the dead may lie has been decided by decrees, epidemics and municipal budgets at least as often as by custom. 1723–present
    Moscow, St Petersburg, Pushkin, provincial Russia     OPEN PLATE →
  7. G 07 The Voice The dead are spoken to. The grammar of that address is one of the most precisely recorded things in Russian ethnography — and one of the least often quoted accurately. Recorded 1872–1886, and later
    Olonets province (Karelia) and the Russian North     OPEN PLATE →
  8. H 08 The Afterlife Orthodox teaching about the dead is precise, and it is not the same thing as the crowded cosmology of the icon. The atlas keeps the two apart, then puts them on the same page. Iconography 15th–18th c.; doctrine and practice to the present
    Novgorod; the Volga region; Russia     OPEN PLATE →
  9. I 09 Collective Death Mass death produces two things at once: a hole in the record and an official number. The atlas is interested in the gap between them. 1771–present
    Moscow, Leningrad/St Petersburg, Volgograd     OPEN PLATE →
  10. J 10 Death in Art Russian art tends to approach death through a single object held at an angle: a coffin on a sled, a square on a wall, a body on a bed. 1865–1935
    Moscow; Nemchinovka     OPEN PLATE →
  11. K 11 The Soviet and Post-Soviet Afterlife The Soviet state kept the funeral and rebuilt it as a ceremony of the collective; then the collective dissolved. 1920s–2020s
    Moscow; St Petersburg; Russia     OPEN PLATE →
  12. L 12 The Unfinished Archive This plate is mostly empty on purpose: an atlas of death that showed everything would be lying about the record. 1930s–present
    Russia; the former Soviet Union     OPEN PLATE →
  13. M 13 The Empty House The plate has four parts. Part I: the house at a death. Part II: the coffin as a house. Part III: the state’s count of villages with no one in them. Part IV: what the three can and cannot say together, which is less than the title suggests. Recorded 19th–20th centuries; census figures 2002–2010
    Eastern Slavic traditions (corpus partly outside Russia); Russian Federation (census)     OPEN PLATE →

N 14 The Forest Edge Three kinds of evidence put the dead near the forest: reports of burial in forest ravines, a belief that the way to the dead leads through the woods, and a fairy-tale hut at the treeline. They come from different peoples and different genres, and the plate does not blend them. Reports published 1916; articles 2025
    Saratov and Simbirsk provinces (reports); Estonia and Belarus (corpus); Russian folktales (reading)     OPEN PLATE →
  15. O 15 Water, Marsh and River In the sources read for this plate, water is the place of the drowned, and the swamp is a border. A calendar day remembered those who died by water; an essay reads swamps as a threshold. The plate keeps the dated custom and the interpretation apart. Customs recorded 19th–20th centuries; essay 2022
    Vyatka, Smolensk and other provinces (Semik reports); Russia and neighbouring peoples (essay)     OPEN PLATE →
  16. P 16 Roads and Crossroads A crossroads is where a village ends, where relatives are seen off, and, in one scholar’s reconstruction, where some of the dead were once buried. A modern field count of roadside memorials gives the plate something firmer: 299 signs on three highways. The plate keeps the old reconstruction and the new count apart. Reconstructed custom; field count 2013–2021
    Russian North (reconstruction); Chuvashia, Tatarstan and Mordovia (count)     OPEN PLATE →
  17. Q 17 The Vanished Village Villages leave the map in two ways: the state floods them, or people leave. The Rybinsk Reservoir covered a town and hundreds of villages; the census records thousands of villages with no residents at all. The plate puts the two side by side and says what is and is not documented about the cemeteries in the flood zone. Flooding 1937–1947; census 2002–2010
    Rybinsk Reservoir (Yaroslavl, Vologda and Tver oblasts); Russian Federation (census)     OPEN PLATE →
  18. R 18 The Underground The plate starts from one documented place: the burial caves of the Pskov-Caves Monastery. It sets what the sources record beside a 2023 muon survey and a legend of a tunnel to Izborsk, and marks which is which. Other underground places stay leads. Caves in use since the fifteenth century; survey 2023
    Pechory, Pskov Oblast, Russian Federation     OPEN PLATE →
  19. S 19 Household Spirits A spirit of the house who is sometimes read as a dead ancestor. The plate sets regional studies side by side (Perm, Arkhangelsk, Kargopol and a multi-region survey), says what each can and cannot carry, and keeps the bannik and the kikimora out of the ancestor story. Field records 1999–2019; recollections from the 1950s
    Russia: Perm Krai and the regions of the 1999–2015 field collections     OPEN PLATE →
  20. T 20 The Unquiet Dead Who was kept out of the common cemetery, by which source and in which decade, with a scholar’s term set apart from what informants said and a church-history challenge to the pagan-survival reading. Records of the 19th–early 20th century; fieldwork 1990s–2019
    Belarusian-Russian borderland: Vitebsk, Pskov and Smolensk regions     OPEN PLATE →
  21. U 21 Calendar of the Dead A table of who named which day of commemoration, in registers kept apart: the Church, the movable Easter calendar, the Komi-Permyak region and today’s cemetery. The parish is an open gap, and the question of origin is left open. Church account; 1995–2016 recordings; fieldwork 2016–2017
    Church calendar; Komi-Permyak districts; Kostroma and Vladimir regions     OPEN PLATE →
  22. V 22 Taboo and Protection What sources report people did in a house of mourning, which reasons are on record for each act, and where the reports do not agree. No customs are offered as rules. Customs reported from earlier ethnography; fieldwork 2018–2019
    East Slavic villages; Russian North; Khislavichi district, Smolensk region     OPEN PLATE →

  1. CON

    Threshold

    The border between the living and the dead, maintained by prohibitions rather than by a wall.

    Used for the set of small adjustments made around a death: the opened window, the covered mirror, the water thrown out, the body carried out. The term is analytical; the sources describe the practices, not the concept.

    A C H E

  2. CON

    Soul

    The part of the person that survives death; described in both theological and folk registers.

    Orthodox doctrine and folk belief use the same word (dusha) for different constructions: a created immortal substance judged by God, and a breath-like presence that lingers near the body and must be fed and escorted. The atlas keeps the registers apart.

    A H G

  3. CON

    Return

    The dead coming back, or being treated as if they might.

    The returning dead are a way of talking about unfinished obligation. The atlas distinguishes the ethnographic reports (documented) from the category built on them (interpretation).

    E A L

  4. CON

    Purification

    The washing of the body and the separation of the living from the dead.

    Washing is both practical and symbolic. The atlas treats it as a technique for handling the dead at arm’s length, and avoids claims about its pre-Christian origin.

    B C

  5. CON

    Mourning

    The social and emotional work of loss, performed in public or withheld.

    In the Russian twentieth century mourning was repeatedly reorganised: by the Church, by the Soviet state and by the family. The atlas follows the distinction between public commemoration and private grief.

    G C I K

  6. CON

    Remembrance

    The scheduled repetition of prayer, food and naming.

    Remembrance in this atlas is a calendar and a set of objects: days three, nine and forty, the annual commemoration, the meal, the name spoken aloud. It is the most documented form of relation with the dead.

    C D H

  7. CON

    Memory

    The use of the dead by the living — personal, familial, national.

    Memory is treated here as a practice with owners and budgets. The state’s memory is built at memorial complexes; the family’s memory at the table and at the grave.

    I F K L

  8. CON

    Abandonment

    The state of a grave, a cemetery or a name that no one maintains.

    Abandonment is what memory turns into when nobody tends it. The atlas documents it as a condition of the material record, and marks its own inability to survey it.

    L F I

  9. CON

    Afterlife

    The imagined geography of the dead.

    The atlas distinguishes the doctrinal claim (judgement, resurrection, communion with God) from the iconographic and folk geography (scales, rivers of fire, roads, places of waiting).

    H A

  10. CON

    Debt

    What the living owe the dead, and what an unpaid obligation is believed to produce.

    Used for the reading, offered on plate E and on the table of plate D, that burial, washing, feeding and commemoration are obligations that can be discharged or left unpaid. The term is analytical; plate E marks the "unpaid debt" reading as the atlas’s own interpretation, not a folk category.

    D E T C

  11. CON

    Calendar of the dead

    The fixed days on which the dead are commemorated, and the ways the church and the village count them differently.

    Covers the third, ninth and fortieth days, the parental Saturdays, Radonitsa, Semik and the Saturdays before Pokrov and St Demetrius. Plates C and U separate the church rule from local custom; the term only groups them.

    C U D O

  12. CON

    Unclean death

    A death outside the expected order, and the burial and fear attached to it.

    Zelenin’s category of the "pledged" dead and the later dispute over it (plates E and T). It is a scholarly construction on real reports, not a taxonomy the sources show villagers sharing.

    E T O N

  13. CON

    Reuse

    The afterlife of stone, timber and ground once the dead are no longer tended.

    Gravestones as curbs and foundations, churches as crematoria, houses left standing. Plates K, W and M record cases; reuse here names a pattern across them, not a single policy.

    K W M F

  14. CON

    Household guardian

    A being tied to a house and its occupants, whose relation to the dead is disputed.

    The domovoy of plate S. Whether it is read as an ancestor is a question the sources answer differently; the atlas keeps the limit of that reading visible.

    S V M A

  15. CON

    Substitution

    One body, name or place standing in for many that cannot be handled individually.

    The unknown soldier, the mass grave, the memorial that carries a figure instead of a register (plates I and L). The term names the exchange of information for ritual focus.

    I L Q J

  16. CON

    Underground

    Burial and passage below the surface, in caves, cellars, tunnels and flooded ground.

    Plates R and Q: the Pechory caves, a legend of a tunnel, drowned settlements. Descriptions of what lies below are marked by whether anything was surveyed or only reported.

    R Q O N

HOW THE INDEX WORKS

Each card is a route. Plates are the long-form compositions; objects, concepts and places are the short entries that recur across them. Selecting a letter on an object card takes you to the plate where that thing is examined.

The theme filter applies to plates only. Objects, concepts and places are filtered by search. Nothing here is ranked; the order is editorial and follows the sequence of plates.
