CHRONOLOGY — POINTS, NOT A TRADITION

THE
TIMELINE

This is not a continuous history of Russian death. It is a line of documents, decrees, publications and buildings, with the breaks left visible.

Chronology

34 ENTRIES

  1. 1380

    PRE-IMPERIAL

    St. Demetrius Saturday is set up

    After the battle of Kulikovo a Saturday of commemoration is instituted, first for the fallen soldiers and later for all the departed, with the blessing of Sergius of Radonezh. This is the Church’s own account of the origin, as a churchman tells it.

    PLATE U — documented (as the Church states it; not independently checked)

  2. 1540s

    PRE-IMPERIAL

    The Last Judgment in Novgorod

    The Last Judgment icon from the Nativity monastery in Novgorod is painted in registers, with the saved and the damned arranged as populations. It is an image made to be read, and it will remain the basic model for Russian judgement pictures for centuries.

    PLATE H — documented (icon date and provenance as recorded by the museum description)

  3. 10 October 1723

    IMPERIAL

    Peter I restricts burial inside towns

    A decree orders that the overcrowded churchyard cemeteries be moved outside the city, with an exception for ‘notable persons’. Pravenc adds that it ordered raised tombs levelled; Rozanov puts that order in an earlier decree of 12 April 1722, and another article dates the burial decree 16 October. The date given here is Pravenc’s and Rozanov’s. After Peter’s death the churchyard burial resumes; the measure is a precedent more than a settlement.

    PLATE F · PLATE K — documented

  4. 1771

    IMPERIAL

    Plague in Moscow; Vagankovo founded

    Tens of thousands die. Grigorii Orlov is sent to Moscow with emergency powers; the dead are buried outside the city, and the new cemetery at Vagankovo begins with unnamed plague victims. Mass death produces the modern out-of-town cemetery.

    PLATE F · PLATE I — documented (the total is not established in the sources used here)

  5. 1771

    IMPERIAL

    Burial in communal pits is banned

    The online encyclopedia entry on Semik says that unidentified and unclaimed bodies were once placed in open communal pits, skudelnitsy, and that Catherine II banned burial in them in 1771. The entry is anonymous and cites no decree.

    PLATE O — documented (anonymous encyclopedia entry; no decree cited)

  6. 1770s–1780s

    IMPERIAL

    The Volga Last Judgment

    An icon of the Last Judgment is painted in the Volga region, 180 × 137 cm. Its compartments and inscribed bands derive from printed engravings: the judgement has become a page.

    PLATE H — documented (date range and provenance per the collection description)

  7. 1803

    IMPERIAL

    Novaia Skrizhal’ published

    Archbishop Veniamin’s handbook sets out the rationale for commemoration on the third, ninth and fortieth days: the Resurrection, the nine ranks of angels, the Ascension. It is the clearest statement of the scheme that later writers would call a folk custom.

    PLATE C · PLATE H — documented (first edition 1803; text consulted in a modern online edition)

  8. 1855–1863

    IMPERIAL

    Afanasyev publishes his folktales

    The collection of Russian folktales on which a 2025 reading of Baba-Yaga’s hut as a threshold rests. The folktales are narrative, not a record of burial practice.

    PLATE N — documented (publication dates as given by Santos Marinas)

  9. 1865

    IMPERIAL

    Perov paints Seeing Off the Dead

    A small canvas, 45.3 × 57 cm, of a family carrying a coffin on a sled. The painting becomes a reference point for the representation of peasant death and is later connected by the gallery to Nekrasov’s poem of 1862–63.

    PLATE J — documented

  10. 1872–1886

    IMPERIAL

    Barsov publishes the northern laments

    Three volumes of laments recorded in the Olonets region (funeral laments in 1872, recruit and soldier laments in 1882, wedding laments in 1886) establish lament as a composed, performed genre with named performers and a repertoire. This is the moment the Russian lament becomes an archive.

    PLATE G — documented

  11. 1881

    IMPERIAL

    A memorial meal photographed

    An unknown photographer records a memorial meal at Smolensk cemetery in St Petersburg. It is one of the few nineteenth-century photographs in this atlas that shows the ritual meal rather than the monument.

    PLATE D — documented (as recorded by the archive description)

  12. 1886

    IMPERIAL

    The Death of Ivan Ilyich

    Tolstoy’s novella turns the deathbed into a corridor. It is not an ethnographic document; it is evidence of how the educated Russian reader was invited to imagine dying.

    PLATE J — documented (work and date); interpretation (reading)

  13. 1916

    IMPERIAL

    Zelenin’s unclean dead

    D. K. Zelenin publishes his study of those who die an unnatural death and of the rusalki, and gives the category zalozhnye pokoiniki its lasting form. The book is a synthesis, and its category is an argument.

    PLATE E — documented

  14. 1917–1922

    SOVIET

    Rupture

    Revolution and civil war break the institutions that had produced most of the sources above: parishes, monasteries, consistories, the ethnographic collections of the imperial academies. After this point, the atlas’s sources change kind.

    PLATE I · PLATE K — interpretation

  15. 1925

    SOVIET

    Moscow holds a design competition for a crematorium

    The Moscow communal administration holds an architectural competition in 1925. The crematorium is later fitted into the church of St Seraphim of Sarov and St Anna of Kashin at the Donskoy Monastery.

    PLATE K — documented (two web accounts; the 1926 magazine itself not seen)

  16. 1927

    SOVIET

    The first Moscow crematorium

    The crematorium opens in the church building at the Donskoy Monastery: 12 January 1927 in Sokolova’s account, a trial burning on 11 January on one hobbyist page. The official opening is 6 October 1927 (Memorial’s Topos database; 7 October on the hobbyist page).

    PLATE K — documented (dates differ by a day between sources; archival check recommended)

  17. 1920–1930

    SOVIET

    Novosibirsk: cemetery grove, church closure, protest

    In the account of E. Krasilnikova, a 1920 decision concerned cutting the grove at the cemetery, the Voskresenskaya church was ordered closed in 1929, and in April 1930 a crowd of about a thousand protested and was dispersed by the militia. The old cemetery became a garden and later part of a park.

    PLATE W — unverified (interviewee’s dated account; no file cited)

  18. 1930s

    SOVIET

    Cemeteries under pressure

    The destruction or truncation of monastic and parish burial grounds in the Soviet period is well attested in general terms, but it has not been itemised by this atlas. The record here is a gap, not a list.

    PLATE F · PLATE L — interpretation; low detail

  19. 1930-е

    SOVIET

    Two large Barnaul cemeteries destroyed

    In Krasilnikova’s account two large cemeteries in Barnaul were destroyed in the 1930s, graves were opened for valuables and remains left scattered. She calls Barnaul the most radical case in Siberia; that comparison is her judgement.

    PLATE W — unverified (interviewee’s account; no file cited)

  20. 1935

    SOVIET

    Malevich buried under a black square

    The painter’s ashes are buried near Nemchinovka under an oak. A wooden cube with a black square, made by Suetin, marks the grave. By one newspaper account the oak is felled in 1941 and the grave is later lost.

    PLATE J · PLATE L — contested (newspaper accounts read as summaries; the later history rests on one article)

  21. 1 August 1936

    SOVIET

    Decree on resettlement from the flood zone

    An article on Cherepovets district states that resettlement followed a decree of the All-Russian Central Executive Committee and the Council of People’s Commissars of that date, and that the residents of about seven hundred villages and hamlets were moved out of the flood zone. The decree itself was not seen.

    PLATE Q — documented (date and decree as stated in an article; decree not seen)

  22. 1941–1944

    SOVIET

    The blockade dead

    Hundreds of thousands die in Leningrad and are buried in mass graves that become the ground of the Piskaryovskoye memorial. The sites are administrative before they are sacred.

    PLATE I — documented (totals contested: about 470,000 and about 520,000 are both reported)

  23. 9 May 1960

    SOVIET

    Piskaryovskoye memorial opened

    The ensemble by A. V. Vasil’ev and E. A. Levinson opens on the fifteenth anniversary of victory. The flame is carried from the Field of Mars, where it had been lit in 1957.

    PLATE I · PLATE K — documented

  24. 8 May 1967

    SOVIET

    The Tomb of the Unknown Soldier

    The monument by Dmitrii Burdin, Vladimir Klimov and Iurii Rabaev, with the sculptor Nikolai Tomskii, opens at the Kremlin wall. An unmanageable number of war dead is converted into one body that can be visited.

    PLATE I · PLATE K — documented

  25. 1991

    POST-SOVIET

    Rupture

    The state monopoly on funeral services ends; commercial funeral homes, municipal fee schedules and, later, online memorials appear. Orthodoxy returns to public ritual. The atlas marks this period as under-researched rather than describing it as a unified new culture of death.

    PLATE K · PLATE L — interpretation

  26. 2005

    POST-SOVIET

    Omsk gathers fragments of its necropolis into a square

    Krasilnikova says the historic necropolis of Omsk survived until the 1950s and was recorded and photographed by a museum director before its demolition. In 2005 fragments of its stones were gathered into a memorial square. Novosibirsk has no comparable memorial.

    PLATE W — unverified (interviewee’s account; no file cited)

  27. 2010

    POST-SOVIET

    The census counts villages with no inhabitants

    Press reports of the 2010 census figures put the number of villages that exist de jure but have no inhabitants at 19,400, up from 13,000, and say 36,200 villages have fewer than ten residents. The reports give figures, not the fate of the cemeteries.

    PLATE M · PLATE Q — documented (press reports of preliminary census figures)

  28. 2013–2021

    POST-SOVIET

    Roadside memorial signs counted on three highways

    M. G. Matlin drives sections of three federal highways in the Middle Volga repeatedly and documents 299 roadside memorial signs, with dates of death from 1965 to 2019. He sorts them into a family that imitates the cemetery and one that does not.

    PLATE P — documented (published survey; three routes, one region)

  29. 2016–2017

    POST-SOVIET

    Fieldwork on cemetery commemoration in Kostroma and Vladimir

    E. G. Chesnokova’s fieldwork finds the main cemetery commemoration falling in the Easter or the Trinity period, following regional zones. Only the abstract was read.

    PLATE U — documented (abstract only; counts of respondents not given)

  30. 2018–2019

    POST-SOVIET

    Fieldwork in Khislavichi district, Smolensk region

    A. Ryko records that informants born in the 1930s remember a ban on taking those who hanged or drowned themselves to the common cemetery, while younger people say they know nothing of it. Neighbours washed the dead, women washing women and men washing men. She says the findings rest on memory and that it is unclear whether the trend is local.

    PLATE T · PLATE V — documented (one district; informants’ memory)

  31. June 2019

    POST-SOVIET

    Domovoy beliefs recorded in Severny Kommunar

    M. A. Granova records beliefs about the domovoy in Severny Kommunar, Sivinsky district, Perm Krai, and compares them with older university-archive material. She finds the beliefs much reduced and gives this as a tendency; the number of informants is not stated.

    PLATE S — documented (one village; informants not counted)

  32. 2019

    POST-SOVIET

    Gravestones found used as curbs on Bersenevskaya Embankment

    A municipal deputy posts photographs of gravestones used as curbs near the Estrada Theatre in Moscow; some stones still show dates of birth and death. The construction department says the work was not under its direction. The find is known through the photographs and an outlet’s report, not through an inspection record.

    PLATE W — documented (deputy’s photographs as reported; no inspection record)

  33. 2020

    POST-SOVIET

    A study of the digital afterlife of Russian internet users

    A. Sergeeva, R. Gerlit and H. Krcmar analyse 33 social networks and online services, survey 173 users and interview experts. Their abstract expects services to host more deceased than living users in the coming decades. This is a projection; only the abstract was read.

    PLATE X — documented (abstract only; sample not known here)

  34. 2023

    POST-SOVIET

    Muon survey in the Pskov-Caves Monastery

    A team lays nuclear-emulsion detectors in the caves at Pechory to image two objects with atmospheric muons. The newspaper account reports a hidden passage of about 12 m behind a walled crypt, a continuation of Trinity Street and a cavity of roughly 3 × 6 × 3 m. These are the team’s estimates as quoted, not checked by opening.

    PLATE R — documented (method and objects); the dimensions are reported estimates

HOW TO READ A BREAK

Red entries are caesuras: moments when the institutions that produced the atlas’s sources changed, and with them the kind of evidence available. They are not events in a story of decline or progress.

Confidence notes appear with every entry. Where a date is a press report rather than an archival record, the entry says so.
