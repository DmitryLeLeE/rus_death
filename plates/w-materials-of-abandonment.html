VOL. IV · PLATE W · 01 / 02

THE MATERIALS OF ABANDONMENT

Demolished cemeteries, gravestones as curbs and what is documented

Dated decisions reported from Siberia and a 2019 find in Moscow, with the larger historical claims marked unverified because the articles cite no documents.

1920–1930s; 1950s; 2005; 2019 · Novosibirsk, Barnaul, Omsk; Moscow · Soviet and post-Soviet treatment of cemeteries

W

W / 01

Novosibirsk: dated decisions, as reported

In a 2023 interview, E. Krasilnikova of Novosibirsk State Technical University dates a run of local decisions. A 1920 decision concerned cutting the grove at the cemetery. In 1929 the Voskresenskaya church was ordered closed, and in April 1930 a crowd of about a thousand protested and was dispersed by the militia. The old cemetery became a garden and later part of a park; the church went to the park administration, held a planetarium and burned in the 1970s.

She places the start of such demolitions in Siberia in the mid-1920s. The interview states the dates as facts but cites no file or archive for them, so they stand here as the interviewee’s report.

W / 02

Barnaul and Omsk

In her account two large cemeteries in Barnaul were destroyed in the 1930s, graves were opened for valuables and remains left scattered. She calls Barnaul the most radical case in Siberia; that comparison is her judgement.

Omsk went another way. She says its historic necropolis survived until the 1950s, was recorded and photographed by a museum director before its demolition, and in 2005 fragments of its stones were gathered into a memorial square. Novosibirsk has no comparable memorial, she adds.

W / 03

Curbs on a Moscow embankment, 2019

A. Minkin reports that gravestones were found used as curbs near the Estrada Theatre on Bersenevskaya Embankment while the curbs were being replaced. The photographs came from a municipal deputy who posted them online, and some stones still showed dates of birth and death. The construction department told the outlet that the work was not under its direction. He also recalls a similar case from 2011 on Bolshaya Polyanka.

The 2019 find is therefore known through a deputy’s photographs and an outlet’s report, not through an inspection record.

W / 04

Claims with no document behind them

The same article quotes an architect, S. Tkachenko, who says that reuse of granite for roads was normal in the 1920s and 1930s, that many stone buildings were demolished in 1934 and the rubble used as metro filler or concrete. The state enterprise ‘Ritual’ is quoted as saying the practice was common, that many cemeteries were cleared in the first half of the twentieth century for the metro and roads, with curbs at Sokolniki, Tverskoy Boulevard and Novaya Basmannaya Street, and that unused slabs were crushed into rubble.

No decision, order or archive is cited for any of this. The atlas keeps the statements and marks them UNVERIFIED: they are plausible, they come from named speakers, and they are not documented here.

W / 05

Why the cemeteries went

Krasilnikova gives four kinds of motive for the demolitions: ideological (shedding pre-revolutionary culture and building a socialist city), practical (neglected graves after the Civil War, typhus burials, no relatives left to tend them), symbolic (local notables buried there whom the new authorities saw no need to remember) and emotional (funerals used to mobilise people, revolutionary rites replacing religious ones).

These are her interpretations of Siberian cases and are not sourced in the interview. The atlas lists them as one reading, and does not give them as the cause of abandoned cemeteries elsewhere, including the one in the photograph.

Caution

What this plate does not say

  • It does not give a total of cemeteries destroyed or gravestones reused. No source read gives one.
  • It does not describe the remains of the people buried in these places, and it shows none.
  • It does not treat Siberian demolitions as the whole of Soviet cemetery policy, or as the explanation for rural cemeteries abandoned through depopulation.
  • It does not repeat tales of individual stones found in pavements that the sources do not tie to a place and a record.
INTERPRETATION

Research leads

What would turn this plate into a study

  • Krasilnikova’s book on the subject, to see which archives stand behind the dates.
  • City council decisions and construction records for Moscow in the 1930s, to test the metro-filler statement.
  • A 2015 proposal of the construction ministry to reuse graves judged abandoned, reported in the press, for the legal side of ‘abandoned’ graves; it needs the draft law and its fate.
  • Studies of village cemeteries abandoned through depopulation, which the sources read here do not cover.
INTERPRETATION

Two articles, one interview. The dates are reported, the motives are one reading, the metro filler is unverified.
