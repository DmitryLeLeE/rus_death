VOL. IV · PLATE I · 01 / 07

COLLECTIVE DEATH

Plague, war, and the state’s arithmetic of the dead

Mass death produces two things at once: a hole in the record and an official number. The atlas is interested in the gap between them.

1771–present · Moscow, Leningrad/St Petersburg, Volgograd · State commemoration; Orthodox and secular rites

I

I / 01

1771: the pit

The Moscow plague of 1771 killed many thousands of people; the municipal note used here gives no total. The dead were buried outside the city, and the new cemetery at Vagankovo received, first of all, unnamed people. The historical account of the cemetery begins with a number it cannot disaggregate.

Mass death produces this kind of record: a pit, a total, a name for the place, no names for the people. The atlas does not illustrate the pit. It prints the fact of it.

Archival demographic studies of the 1770–1772 Moscow plague estimate total mortality between 50,000 and 100,000 victims, precipitating a permanent transformation of Russian burial geography. The Senate decrees of 24 March and 1 November 1771 marked the first absolute civil prohibition against churchyard interments in Russian cities. To prevent pestilence, the municipal administration mandated the creation of dedicated burial grounds beyond the Kamer-Kollezhsky customs rampart—founding Vagankovo, Danilovskoe, Miusskoe, and Rogozhskoe as mass sanitary burial tracts where thousands of plague dead were interred in common lime-dusted trenches.

I / 02

The unknown soldier

The Tomb of the Unknown Soldier at the Kremlin wall was opened on 8 May 1967. The monument was designed by the architects Dmitrii Burdin, Vladimir Klimov and Iurii Rabaev, with the sculptor Nikolai Tomskii; the remains had been brought from a mass grave outside Moscow. The eternal flame was lit at the opening and has been maintained since.

The unknown soldier is a very specific solution to a problem of scale: it converts an unmanageable number into a single body that can be visited. What it gains in ritual focus it loses in information. The state’s memory is built on that trade.

I / 03

Unburied

Alexander Etkind’s Warped Mourning argues that Soviet culture was shaped by deaths that were not buried properly — the executed, the disappeared, the dead in unmarked ground — and that literature and memory-work in Russia have repeatedly returned to them as the ‘undead’. The argument is a cultural history, not a survey of graves, and the atlas presents it as an interpretation.

Catherine Merridale’s Night of Stone makes a related point from the side of the living: the twentieth century in Russia produced so much death that mourning itself became a skill that had to be managed, suppressed, or performed in private. Both books are used here for their arguments, not as inventories of fact.

I / 03

‘Buried well’: a village formula and the funeral photograph

Oleg Nikolaev, a philologist at the Memorial society, wrote in 2012 about funeral photographs in Russian family albums. From his fieldwork he reports that in some archives they make up to 20% of all pictures, and that they peak in the 1950s–1970s: at the open coffin, on the lorry to the cemetery, at the grave, at the memorial table. He thinks the practice belongs to villages, small provincial towns and city people of village origin, and that it had largely left use by the 1980s (pp. 358–359, 361, 369–370). The three photographs reproduced in the article come from family archives: one from Bashkortostan (a funeral of the 1930s) and two from a village in the Chelyabinsk region (pp. 359–361).

His central observation is a phrase. In his account, a funeral is judged by the formula ‘buried well’ (pokhoronili khorosho), and stories about funerals are usually built on it and most often begin or end with it. It covers correct ritual from washing to the memorial meal, a good place near relatives, a tended grave, telegrams sent to the family and many people coming to take leave, and also that the grief was told and heard (p. 365). He found the same phrase in a soldier’s letter of 25 September 1943, where a comrade tells a widow that her husband was buried well and that the grave was edged with turf and a marker set up; the letter is held in the digital archive of the ‘Museum of Folk Writing’ programme (p. 368). He also prints in full a written account of 1984 by his grandfather, Fyodor Vinogradov, of the death and burial of his wife, and reads it as the formula unfolded point by point (pp. 365–367).

From this he argues that the photograph served the same formula in visual form. His reasoning: after the war, deportation and the camps many families knew of relatives who had gone missing or lain in mass or nameless graves, and a photograph proved that this family could bury its own dead, and bury them correctly. Prints were sent to relatives in other towns, which let a scattered kin group see who stood by the coffin (pp. 368–369). This is his hypothesis, put forward as a supposition, and it rests on his own expedition collections, which the article does not list or count. The plate uses it as an interpretation beside Etkind and Merridale, and does not treat it as a measured fact.

I / 03

Trinity Saturday at a mass grave: Yurla district, Perm region, 2013–2015

A team from Perm State University (Svetlana Koroleva, Elena Chetina, Oksana Kolegova) gathered interviews and photographs in the Yurla district of the Perm region, a Russian-speaking area among Komi-Permyaks, in 2013–2015. Their article describes how places linked to the Civil War and to the settling of special deportees (1919 to the early 1950s) came into local commemoration. Their finding is that the places fall on a spectrum between official and popular forms, and that where the popular form prevails the mass grave is placed in the calendar commemoration of one’s own dead, which turns from a family rite into a shared one.

Their clearest case is Ust-Pyshya, a logging settlement founded in the late 1940s or early 1950s that ceased to exist in 2014. Until then its residents held a multi-stage commemoration in Trinity week: first the graves of their relatives at the cemetery near the village of Bulychi, then the obelisk at the mass grave of fourteen people shot there by Whites in 1919, whose names are mostly unknown, and then a cemetery of Polish special settlers, always in nearly the same order. All informants the authors spoke to said they went to the grave on Trinity Saturday and not on revolutionary holidays. They censed their own relatives’ graves, then those of people with no one to commemorate, then the obelisk, brought food, and called the dead simply the ‘killed’. One woman explained the visits by saying that nobody else commemorated them. The authors also report that other Yurla places linked to bloodshed or abandoned cemeteries were understood as ‘unclean’ and dangerous.

The evidence is interview recollection in one district, with the authors’ own count of informants not stated in the part read, and the narrators of the mythological stories are withheld by the authors’ own note. It shows a documented case of an ordinary calendar rite extended to the war dead of a settlement that no longer exists. It cannot tell how common the practice is in Russia; the plate keeps it beside Nikolaev’s village formula as a second regional observation.

Why there are no bodies here

  • This plate uses no photographs of corpses, no photographs of starving people, no images of the blockade dead. The atlas treats these as deaths, not as imagery.
  • The monuments are shown because they are what the state built, and because their surfaces are evidence of how memory was organised.

I / 08

Butovo: a count with four numbers

The Butovo firing range south of Moscow is the plate’s case of mass death that was administered, not suffered by accident. A memorial site run by the church says it is the largest place of mass shootings and burials of victims of Stalinist repression in the Moscow region, that 20,760 names are known, that the shootings fell between August 1937 and October 1938, and that the range worked from 1934 to 1953. The Great Russian Encyclopedia, read only in extract, gives about 20.8 thousand executed and buried in those months, of them 19.9 thousand men, over 80 per cent non-party. Other pages read give 20,761, 20,762 and 20,765. The atlas does not choose among them. The spread is small. The figure rests on execution lists and case files, so it exists because the state kept records, the reverse of the 1771 pit. No remains are shown.

I / 09

The procession of portraits

Collective death is also remembered from below, and the best-documented recent case began in Siberia. According to Meduza, on 9 May 2012 six thousand people in Tomsk walked with portraits of their relatives who fought in the war; the form was taken up in about 140 places in 2013 and about 450 in 2014. The same article is mainly about a dispute over who owns the name and the procession, which this plate does not follow. What matters here is the object: a photograph carried on a stick through a city, one dead person to one living carrier. It is the opposite of the unknown soldier earlier on this plate, which merges many into one; here the many are kept apart, each with a face and a relative.

Nekrasov wrote the poem in 1855, during the Crimean War, and published it in 1856. It is not a soldier's song or a funeral lament. It is a literary argument that the true grief of war belongs to mothers, whose tears cannot be consoled, while wives and friends forget.

The poem is used in the plate on collective death as a counter to the state's monumental memory. It insists on the one mourner who does not forget, and it compares her to a weeping willow that cannot lift its branches. The «bloody field» is a literary generalization; the poem gives no place, date or name.

кровавая нива
Bloody field; literally a field of grain, used as a metaphor of battle.

Surikov's poem was written in 1869 and revised in 1877. It became one of the most widely sung Russian songs about a death on the road: a coachman dying in the steppe gives his comrade instructions for burial, for the horses, for his parents and for his young wife. The poem does not name a province; the ‘Mozdok steppe’ locates it in the North Caucasus, on the road, not in a village.

The song is literary in origin, not a recorded folk lament. Its instructions — bury me here, take the horses home, tell my wife not to wait — are close to the formulas of a death on the road, but they are Surikov's composition. Use it as a literary song, not as a transcript of a peasant funeral.

ямщик
A coachman, a driver of a horse-drawn carriage or sled.
Моздокская степь
The steppe around Mozdok, in the North Caucasus; a named route, not a generic ‘Russian’ landscape.
обручальное кольцо
Betrothal/wedding ring; the dying man returns it so that his wife is free to marry.

A Cossack song about a wounded man who answers the raven circling over him. The first three stanzas refuse the bird its prey; the last accepts it. The ‘marriage’ is to an arrow in battle, and the bloody handkerchief is to be carried to the beloved. The song is built on a single address and a reversal, not on a narrative.

The literary source is usually identified as Veryovkin's poem of 1831, but the text printed here is a folk variant. The Wikisource page names no songbook. Use it as a song text, not as a record of a specific funeral; the raven is an image of death, not a belief.

калена стрела
A tempered arrow; ‘калёная’ means hardened by heat.
любушка
Dear love, sweetheart; a folk diminutive.
венчала
Married (in the church rite); here used metaphorically of the arrow that kills him.
